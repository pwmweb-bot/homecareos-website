The research behind homecareOS

We build features on evidence, not hunches. Below are 49 peer-reviewed journal papers, grouped by the feature they inform, that shaped how homecareOS schedules visits, records care, manages medication, and supports carers. Every one is cited and linked so you can read it yourself.

How we selected these. Each feature was matched to its research literature, and every paper was independently checked (exact-title search plus DOI resolution) to confirm it is real and that we describe its findings accurately.

We report the evidence honestly. Findings are summarised as the papers actually state them, including caveats (some evidence is from care homes or hospitals rather than domiciliary care) and, where it exists, evidence that doesn't support a simple claim. Good software decisions come from reading the whole picture, not cherry-picking.

Bradford Factor & absence management

homecareOS calculates the Bradford Factor (S squared x D) natively with RAG thresholds (0-99 green, 100-199 amber, 200+ red) for carer absence/retention management.

Cohort / observational study
Prediction tools for sickness absenteeism
Merekoulias G, Alexopoulos EC, 2015, International Journal of Workplace Health Management, 8(2):142-151

In a retrospective study of shipyard employees (absence data 2002-2006) combined with the Greek Work Ability Index, higher Bradford Factor values were positively and strongly correlated (p<0.001) with greater sickness absence in the following years, and combining the Bradford Factor with the Work Ability Index predicted future absence better than either measure alone.

Why it matters for homecareOS: This is the most directly on-point peer-reviewed evidence that the Bradford Factor has genuine predictive validity as an early-warning / trigger tool, exactly the use homecareOS makes of it by surfacing BF scores with RAG thresholds so coordinators can act proactively on carers at risk of future absence.
Read the paper →
Cohort / observational study
Risk of future sickness absence in frequent and long-term absentees
Koopmans PC, Roelen CAM, Groothoff JW, 2008, Occupational Medicine (Oxford), 58(4):268-274

In a four-year longitudinal cohort of roughly 53,990 Dutch postal and telecom employees, frequent absentees had markedly elevated risk of recurrent absence (rate ratios ~4.9 in men and ~3.2 in women versus the reference population) and an increased risk of progressing to long-term absence.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Provides the large-cohort empirical basis for weighting absence frequency (the S² term in the Bradford Factor) by showing that people with many short spells are the ones most likely to have further and long-term absence, which is precisely the population the homecareOS amber/red thresholds are meant to flag.
Read the paper →
Review
How to measure sickness absence? Literature review and suggestion of five basic measures
Hensing G, Alexanderson K, Allebeck P, Bjurulf P, 1998, Scandinavian Journal of Social Medicine, 26(2):133-144

A literature review found that the terminology and measures used in sickness-absence research varied widely across studies, and proposed five standardised basic measures (frequency, length, incidence rate, cumulative incidence and duration) as a common epidemiological framework.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Foundational sickness-absence measurement reference establishing that frequency and duration (the two inputs S and D of the Bradford Factor) are the core, accepted dimensions of absence measurement, grounding the metric's construction in recognised methodology rather than ad hoc HR practice.
Read the paper →
Analysis
Measuring the Illness-Related Absenteeism: Can Bradford Factor Approximate the Indirect Cost of Illness?
Koń B, Jakubczyk M, 2023, Central European Journal of Economic Modelling and Econometrics, 2023, issue 3, pp. 241-265

This econometric modelling study examined whether the Bradford Factor can stand in for the indirect (productivity-loss) cost of illness and found that the two are associated only in a non-linear way, so indirect cost cannot be derived directly from a Bradford Factor score.

Why it matters for homecareOS: A useful caveat/critique for the feature: it supports using the Bradford Factor as a relative flag but warns against reading it as a precise measure of the operational or financial cost of absence, a reason to treat the homecareOS RAG bands as triage signals and conversation triggers, not exact cost or performance judgements.

Continuity of care

homecareOS AI cover finder scores available carers with continuity weighted 25% and a preferred-carer flag, to keep the same carers with the same service users.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Continuity of care interventions for preventing hospital readmission of older people with chronic diseases: A meta-analysis
Facchinetti G, D'Angelo D, Piredda M, Petitti T, Matarese M, Oliveti A, De Marinis MG, 2020, International Journal of Nursing Studies

In a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials, continuity-of-care interventions significantly reduced short-term (30-day) hospital readmission of older people with chronic diseases, though evidence for reducing long-term readmission was inconclusive.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Strongest (meta-analysis of RCTs) evidence that deliberately engineering continuity of care reduces avoidable hospital readmissions in the older, chronically-ill population homecareOS serves, supporting continuity as a scored objective rather than a nicety.
Read the paper →
Systematic review / meta-analysis
Impact of relational continuity of primary care in aged care: a systematic review
Dyer SM, Suen J, Williams H, Inacio MC, Harvey G, Roder D, Wesselingh S, Kellie A, Crotty M, Caughey GE, 2022, BMC Geriatrics

Across 5 included studies (1 stepped-wedge cluster RCT), greater relational continuity with a primary care professional probably reduces hospital admissions for community-dwelling aged-care recipients by roughly 4-6% (moderate-certainty evidence); benefit in residential settings and effects on mortality/ADL were uncertain, and satisfaction was not measured with validated scales.

Why it matters for homecareOS: A recent systematic review specifically on relational continuity in aged care (including home/community settings) showing continuity plausibly lowers hospitalisation, directly underpinning why the cover finder weights keeping the same caregiver with the same person. Reported honestly: the effect is modest and the satisfaction evidence here is weak.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
A systematic review on the effectiveness of continuity of care and its role in patient satisfaction and decreased hospital readmissions in the adult patient receiving home care services
Santomassino M, Costantini GD, McDermott M, Primiano D, Slyer JT, Singleton JK, 2012, JBI Library of Systematic Reviews

This systematic review of adults receiving home care services concluded that consistently scheduled home care services promote patient satisfaction, and that continuity through specialist nursing plus interdisciplinary collaboration contributed to reduced hospital readmission rates in high-risk populations.

Why it matters for homecareOS: The most directly on-topic review for domiciliary/home care specifically, linking scheduling consistency to patient satisfaction, the satisfaction pillar of the cover finder's continuity rationale. Note: an older JBI review with heterogeneous included studies, so it is supportive rather than definitive.
Read the paper →
Cohort / observational study
Continuity of Nursing Care in Home Health: Impact on Rehospitalization Among Older Adults With Dementia
Ma C, McDonald MV, Feldman PH, Miner S, Jones S, Squires A, 2021, Medical Care

In a cohort of 23,886 people with dementia receiving post-hospital home health care, lower and moderate continuity of nursing care were associated with significantly higher 30-day rehospitalization versus high continuity (adjusted OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.25-1.46 for low; adjusted OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.22-1.43 for moderate), after adjusting for clinical risk and visit intensity.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Large observational study measuring caregiver-continuity almost exactly as the cover finder operationalises it (dispersion of nurses across a series of home visits), showing ~30% higher readmission odds when continuity is low, a concrete, quantified justification for the 25% continuity weight, in a dementia population highly represented in UK home care.

Cohort / observational study
Continuity in home health care: is consistency in nursing personnel associated with better patient outcomes?
Russell D, Rosati RJ, Rosenfeld P, Marren JM, 2011, Journal for Healthcare Quality

In a cohort of 59,854 home health patients, greater consistency in the nursing personnel delivering a patient's visits was associated with a lower probability of hospitalization and emergent care, and a higher likelihood of improved activities-of-daily-living functioning between admission and discharge.

Why it matters for homecareOS: A large home-health cohort directly testing 'same carer' consistency across a run of visits, the exact behaviour the cover finder and preferred-carer flag are designed to maximise, and linking it to fewer hospitalisations and better functional outcomes.

Home care scheduling, rostering & travel optimisation

homecareOS AI Rota Builder and cover finder optimise scheduling (distance 30%, hours headroom 20%, schedule fit 10%) for domiciliary rounds.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Operational research applied to decisions in home health care: A systematic literature review
Grieco L, Utley M, Crowe S, 2020, Journal of the Operational Research Society (Vol. 72, Issue 9, pp. 1960-1991)

A systematic literature review (from UCL's Clinical Operational Research Unit) mapping how operational research is used across strategic, tactical and operational home-care decisions (including districting, workforce capacity, caregiver-to-patient assignment, and the routing/scheduling of visits) and highlighting a persistent gap between published OR models and their real-world implementation in practice.

Why it matters for homecareOS: This is the strongest single evidence anchor for homecareOS's scheduling engine: it is a UK-authored systematic review confirming that assignment, routing and scheduling of home-care visits are established OR decision problems, and its implementation-gap warning supports building the optimiser into a working operational tool rather than as a standalone model.
Read the paper →
Review
Home health care routing and scheduling: A review
Fikar C, Hirsch P, 2017, Computers & Operations Research (Vol. 77, pp. 86-95)

A widely cited review that frames home health care routing and scheduling as an extension of the vehicle routing problem with side constraints (time windows, working-time limits, qualification/continuity requirements), and classifies the solution literature into exact, heuristic and metaheuristic methods, with travel-time/distance minimisation among the most common objectives.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Provides the foundational academic basis for homecareOS weighting distance (30%) and schedule fit (10%): it establishes that minimising carer travel while respecting visit time windows and working-hour constraints is the core, well-studied objective of the HHCRSP that the AI Rota Builder solves.

Review
Home healthcare routing and scheduling: operations research approaches and contemporary challenges
Masmoudi M, Euchi J, Siarry P, 2024, Annals of Operations Research (Vol. 343, Issue 2, pp. 701-751)

A recent comprehensive review synthesising OR approaches to the home healthcare routing and scheduling problem (drawing on ~112 references), covering deterministic, stochastic/robust and dynamic formulations and identifying contemporary challenges such as uncertain service/travel times, continuity of care, and real-time re-scheduling.

Why it matters for homecareOS: The most up-to-date review confirming the field homecareOS operates in; its treatment of dynamic re-scheduling and uncertainty directly maps to the cover finder's need to reallocate carers at short notice, and its continuity-of-care theme supports the preferred-carer/continuity weighting alongside distance and hours headroom.

Review
Routing and scheduling in Home Health Care: A literature survey and bibliometric analysis
Di Mascolo M, Martinez C, Espinouse M-L, 2021, Computers & Industrial Engineering (Vol. 158, article 107255)

A literature survey and bibliometric analysis of home health care routing and scheduling research that classifies studies by problem features, constraints (e.g. time windows, caregiver skills, continuity, workload balancing) and objective functions, and documents the rapid growth and clustering of publications in this area.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Its taxonomy of objectives and constraints validates homecareOS's multi-criteria scoring design: combining travel/distance, workload/hours-headroom balancing and schedule fit is exactly the constraint set the survey catalogues as standard in the field.

Review
OR problems related to Home Health Care: A review of relevant routing and scheduling problems
Cissé M, Yalçındağ S, Kergosien Y, Şahin E, Lenté C, Matta A, 2017, Operations Research for Health Care (Vol. 13-14, pp. 1-22)

A structured review of the OR routing and scheduling problems arising in home health care, describing the assignment of caregivers to patients, the sequencing and timing of visits, and the modelling of constraints such as time windows, caregiver availability/qualifications and continuity of care, together with the objective functions and solution methods reported in the literature.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Reinforces the academic legitimacy of the cover finder and rota builder by cataloguing caregiver-to-patient assignment plus visit routing/scheduling, including availability and hours constraints that correspond to homecareOS's hours-headroom (20%) weighting, as core, well-defined OR problems in home care.

eMAR / electronic medication administration records

homecareOS eMAR: scheduled + PRN, controlled-drug double-sign, refusals, photo capture, reconciliation, real-time oversight.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Medication administration in aged care facilities: A mixed-methods systematic review
Garratt S, Dowling A, Manias E, 2025, Journal of Advanced Nursing (Wiley)

In this mixed-methods systematic review of medication administration in aged care facilities, the three intervention studies that moved from paper to electronic medication administration records reported significantly reduced medication errors and incidents, alongside better documentation, secure timestamping and faster pharmacist review (paper records could be altered after the fact, slow to update and hard to read).

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly supports the core homecareOS eMAR claim that replacing paper MAR with a digital record reduces administration errors in long-term/aged care; its emphasis on tamper-evident timestamped records and easy retrieval maps onto eMAR reconciliation and real-time oversight.
Read the paper →
Systematic review / meta-analysis
How effective are electronic medication systems in reducing medication error rates and associated harm among hospital inpatients? A systematic review and meta-analysis
Gates PJ, Hardie RA, Raban MZ, Li L, Westbrook JI, 2021, Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (Oxford Academic)

Pooled meta-analysis of controlled studies found electronic medication systems reduced medication administration errors, with a pooled relative risk of 0.77 (95% CI 0.72-0.83), roughly a 23% reduction, and consistent reductions in prescribing errors; however, a meta-analysis of 5 studies found no significant reduction in errors causing actual patient harm.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Provides the strongest study design (systematic review + meta-analysis) quantifying that electronic medication administration cuts administration-error rates, underpinning eMAR's safety-check/alert logic. Caveat included honestly: this evidence is from hospital inpatients, not care homes, and harm reduction was not demonstrated.
Read the paper →
Cohort / observational study
Medication administration errors for older people in long-term residential care
Szczepura A, Wild D, Nelson S, 2011, BMC Geriatrics (Springer/BMC)

A prospective observational study of a pharmacy-managed barcode-assisted electronic medication administration system rolled out across 13 UK care homes captured 2,289 potential administration errors among 188,249 administrations (about 1.2%), with 90% of residents exposed to at least one potential error and roughly half to a serious error (e.g. wrong resident); wrong-time attempts were the commonest (45%). The electronic barcode system detected and flagged these in real time.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Large real-world UK care-home evidence that an electronic barcode eMAR intercepts high-risk administration errors (wrong resident, wrong time) that paper MAR would miss, the exact mechanism behind homecareOS eMAR's right-resident/right-time checks, photo/scan capture and real-time oversight. Note: it demonstrates error interception by the electronic system rather than a head-to-head error-rate comparison against paper.
Read the paper →
Cohort / observational study
The impact of electronic medication administration records in a residential aged care home
Qian S, Yu P, Hailey DM, 2015, International Journal of Medical Informatics (Elsevier)

An observational time-motion study comparing an eMAR unit with a paper-MAR unit in an Australian residential aged care home found the electronic record prevented double-signing errors and the paper-based procedural violation of documenting before administering, and reduced the risk of forgotten medications, though it introduced new unintended consequences (e.g. delayed loading of a new resident's medication profile and omissions during device power outages). No significant difference in medication-round time was detected.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Direct paper-vs-electronic comparison in a residential care setting supporting eMAR's controlled-drug double-sign and administer-then-sign controls, while realistically flagging setup/availability failure modes homecareOS must mitigate (offline-first, timely profile loading).

Review
Electronic Medication Administration Records in Long-Term Care Facilities: A Scoping Review
Fuller AEC, Guirguis LM, Sadowski CA, Makowsky MJ, 2018, Journal of the American Geriatrics Society (Wiley)

A scoping review of 34 studies (2006-2016) of eMAR in long-term care found reported benefits for documentation and workflow, but concluded that the evidence specifically linking eMAR use to reductions in medication errors is weak because of suboptimal study design and reporting, and that standalone eMAR (without barcode/decision support) has limited proven impact on administration-error rates.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Included for balance and credibility: it tempers the error-reduction claim and argues that eMAR delivers the most safety benefit when combined with barcode scanning and decision support/alerts, validating homecareOS's design choice to pair the record with scan/photo capture, safety checks and real-time oversight rather than a bare digital chart.

Digital care records at point of care (offline mobile)

homecareOS carer app records care notes, body maps, observations and check-in/out offline at the point of care, syncing when back online.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Best Practices for Implementing Electronic Care Records in Adult Social Care: Rapid Scoping Review
Snow M, Silva-Ribeiro W, Baginsky M, Di Giorgio S, Farrelly N, Larkins C, Poole K, Steils N, Westwood J, Malley J, 2025, JMIR Aging (JMIR Publications), vol 8, e60107

Rapid scoping review of 29 studies on electronic/digital care records in adult social care: improved efficiency, accuracy and time savings were the most commonly reported positive outcomes (with workarounds and added time burdens the most common negatives), and the review catalogued 45 facilitators and 102 barriers to implementation, noting only 17 of 29 studies actually reported outcomes.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly scopes the adult social care evidence base for the exact class of system homecareOS is (digital care records), confirming that efficiency/accuracy/time-saving benefits are real but contingent on good implementation, a defensible, honest evidence anchor for the point-of-care records feature.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Impact of Electronic Health Records on Long-Term Care Facilities: Systematic Review
Kruse CS, Mileski M, Vijaykumar AG, Viswanathan SV, Suskandla U, Chidambaram Y, 2017, JMIR Medical Informatics (JMIR Publications), vol 5(3):e35

Systematic review concluding that EHR implementation in long-term care facilities improved the management of clinical documentation and enabled better decision-making; roughly 33% of papers showed positive quality outcomes while ~43% showed mixed impact, and evidence on satisfaction, length of stay and productivity was sparse.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Provides higher-tier (systematic review) evidence that electronic records improve documentation management and decision support in a care (non-hospital) setting, supporting homecareOS's claim that structured digital records raise record quality, while honestly signalling mixed/limited effects on some outcomes.

Qualitative study

Malley J, Westwood J, Snow M, Farrelly N, Steils N, Henderson C, Larkins C, 2026, Health and Social Care Delivery Research (NIHR Journals Library)

NIHR mixed-methods rapid evaluation across 30 care providers in England (19 care homes and 11 home care agencies; interviews with 30 senior leaders, 30 care staff and 23 people drawing on care/relatives) found benefits including increased accuracy of record-keeping (better evidence) and more timely, accessible records improving efficiency and safety, but implementation was too often suboptimal due to poor supplier choice and inadequate planning, resourcing and accessibility.

Why it matters for homecareOS: The most directly on-point UK evidence: an independent NIHR evaluation that explicitly includes home care agencies and reports accuracy, timeliness, efficiency and safety gains from digital social care records, mirroring homecareOS's value proposition (and flagging the supplier/implementation risks it must avoid).

Cohort / observational study
Impact of Homecare Electronic Health Record on Timeliness of Clinical Documentation, Reimbursement, and Patient Outcomes
Sockolow PS, Bowles KH, Adelsberger MC, Chittams JL, Liao C, 2014, Applied Clinical Informatics (Schattauer/Thieme), vol 5(2):445-462

Before-after study in a US home care agency: after EHR implementation 90% of notes were completed within a 1-day compliance interval versus only 30% within 7 days pre-implementation (odds ratio 19, p<0.001), and time to Medicare claims fell from ~100 to ~30 days; effects on clinical patient outcomes were limited.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Quantitative home care evidence that point-of-care electronic documentation dramatically improves the timeliness of care records and downstream billing, directly supporting homecareOS's check-in/out and point-of-care note capture, while tempering claims about direct clinical-outcome improvement.

Cohort / observational study

Perez-Marti M, Casado-Marin L, Guillen-Villar A, 2022, JMIR Human Factors (JMIR Publications), vol 9(1):e30512

Before-after time-motion study (130 observation sessions) found that making the electronic record available at the point of care on tablets reduced the time nurses spent on records on most shifts, because transcription, latency time and moving to a workstation were no longer necessary (no time reduction was seen on the night shift).

Why it matters for homecareOS: Evidence for the specific mechanism behind homecareOS's point-of-care mobile capture: entering data on a mobile device where care happens removes transcription and back-to-desk steps, saving documentation time and avoiding the transcription errors introduced by later re-keying. Note: this study is a hospital internal-medicine unit, not home care, so it is supporting rather than directly domiciliary evidence.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly supports the family portal's core mechanic of pushing visit and care-summary information to family members, showing that structured visit-information sharing measurably improves how prepared, confident and engaged family caregivers feel.

Workforce retention, turnover & wellbeing

homecareOS nightly retention risk scoring plus carer wellbeing signals aim to reduce turnover in home care.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Factors Impacting Retention of Aged Care Workers: A Systematic Review
Thwaites C, McKercher JP, Fetherstonhaugh D, Blackberry I, Gilmartin-Thomas JF-M, Taylor NF, Bourke SL, Fowler-Davis S, Hammond S, Morris ME, 2023, Healthcare (Basel)

A mixed-methods systematic review of 49 studies (JBI convergent integrated approach, 2012-2023) found retention of aged/home care workers is driven by individual factors (personal role satisfaction, positive relationships with staff/families/clients, cooperative culture) and organisational factors (career development, appropriate wages, injury-prevention policies, job stability), while understaffing, heavy workloads, stress and low job satisfaction drive people out.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Provides the highest-tier synthesised evidence for which factors a retention model should track; it validates that turnover is multi-factorial (workload, satisfaction, wages, relationships) rather than reducible to a single signal, supporting a composite risk score fed by workload/schedule and wellbeing inputs.
Read the paper →
Systematic review / meta-analysis
Eldercare's Turnover Intention and Human Resource Approach: A Systematic Review
Jurij R, Ismail IR, Alavi K, Alavi R, 2023, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health

A systematic review of 29 publications (2015-2021, ROSES protocol, six databases) identified three primary drivers of eldercare workers' turnover intention - job burnout (physical/emotional fatigue from heavy workloads and shift rotation), low job motivation (poor pay and limited advancement), and restricted job autonomy/lack of managerial support - and proposed HR responses around training, compensation/security and workplace culture.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly names burnout, workload/shift patterns and low autonomy as the leading turnover-intention signals, which is the causal basis for surfacing carer wellbeing and scheduling-load signals in a nightly retention risk score.
Read the paper →
Cohort / observational study
Burnout in UK care home staff and its effect on staff turnover: MARQUE English national care home longitudinal survey
Costello H, Cooper C, Marston L, Livingston G, 2019, Age and Ageing

In a naturalistic longitudinal cohort of 2,062 care staff across 97 English care home units followed for 12 months, nearly 30% had high burnout on at least one Maslach dimension and median annual turnover was 22.7%, but the study found NO association between individual staff burnout level and subsequent turnover; the only significant turnover predictor was nursing- (vs residential-) home status.

Why it matters for homecareOS: An important cautionary UK data point: it shows measured burnout did not predict who actually left, warning that a retention model must not treat a wellbeing/burnout signal as a standalone turnover predictor and should combine it with organisational and workload factors. Finding is reported faithfully - this study is a null result on burnout-to-turnover.
Read the paper →
Cohort / observational study
Determinants of longer job tenure among home care aides: what makes some stay on the job while others leave?
Butler SS, Brennan-Ing M, Wardamasky S, Ashley A, 2014, Journal of Applied Gerontology

A mixed-methods longitudinal study following 261 US home care aides over 18 months found predictors of longer job tenure included older age, rural residence, higher wages and a greater sense of job autonomy; qualitatively, long-term stayers were less troubled by low wages and inconsistent hours than early leavers, though both groups reported high job satisfaction.

Why it matters for homecareOS: One of few home-care-specific longitudinal studies on who stays versus leaves; identifies wages, autonomy and hours consistency as tenure predictors - concrete, individual-level features a per-carer retention risk score could operationalise.

Qualitative study
Zero-hour contracts and stress in UK domiciliary care workers
Ravalier J, Morton R, Russell L, Fidalgo AR, 2019, Health & Social Care in the Community

A qualitative thematic-analysis study of 29 semi-structured interviews with West Midlands domiciliary care workers (15 zero-hours, 14 guaranteed-hours) found all workers faced stressors of poor pay, work-life imbalance and low peer support, with zero-hours workers additionally burdened by income/scheduling uncertainty and management power imbalances; positive service-user relationships were the main stress reliever.

Why it matters for homecareOS: UK-specific evidence on the exact home care context homecareOS serves, pinpointing pay, scheduling uncertainty and lack of guaranteed hours as stress and attrition drivers - signals a rota/scheduling platform can detect (e.g. erratic or low guaranteed hours) and feed into a retention risk model.

Electronic call monitoring / GPS visit verification

homecareOS GPS check-in/out verifies visit attendance and timing.

Survey
When Caring and Surveillance Technology Meet: Organizational Commitment and Discretionary Effort in Home Care Work
Brown K, Korczynski M, 2010, Work and Occupations (SAGE), 37(3):404-432

In a mixed-methods study of home care workers (survey with regression analysis plus interviews), perceiving IT/electronic surveillance as a controlling force and management as hindering client service was associated with lower organizational commitment, yet workers sustained discretionary effort to keep delivering meaningful care to clients.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly informs how GPS/electronic check-in is experienced as a monitoring/control mechanism: it can erode workers' commitment to the employer even as they keep working hard for clients. Suggests homecareOS should frame verification as attendance assurance/safety rather than surveillance-for-control, or risk the same commitment cost.

Qualitative study
Care in a Time of Austerity: the Electronic Monitoring of Homecare Workers' Time
Hayes LJB, Moore S, 2017, Gender, Work & Organization (Wiley), 24(4):329-344

UK qualitative study finds electronic monitoring (ECM) let local authorities commission and pay for homecare on the basis of measured 'contact time' inside the client's home only, materially reducing workers' paid time and wages by excluding travel and time between visits, and reconstructing care as a mere consumption of time.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Core UK evidence that GPS/call-monitored 'time in the home' can be used to shrink what carers are paid for. A cautionary reference for homecareOS: verified visit timing must not become the sole basis for pay, and the platform's four-value time model (planned/actual/paid/charged) plus paid travel/gap handling is exactly the safeguard this literature says is missing.

Qualitative study
Taking worker productivity to a new level? Electronic Monitoring in homecare - the (re)production of unpaid labour
Moore S, Hayes LJB, 2017, New Technology, Work and Employment (Wiley), 32(2):101-114

UK empirical study argues that electronic monitoring combined with zero-hours contracts formalises the boundary between paid and unpaid work and, under constrained council budgets, 'squeezes out' paid travel, time between visits, training and supervision by anchoring cost to minute-by-minute time spent in clients' homes.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Explains the mechanism by which minute-accurate electronic verification can increase unpaid labour. Reinforces that homecareOS should pay travel and between-visit time as first-class, independent of charged 'contact time', rather than letting GPS timing default to a pay-shrinking metric.

Qualitative study
Working against the clock: digital surveillance in US Medicaid homecare services
Mateescu A, 2024, Journal of Sociology (SAGE), 60(3):560-576

Qualitative interviews with US Medicaid homecare workers and clients find that Electronic Visit Verification (EVV) using GPS and biometric check-ins, though justified as fraud prevention, produces detailed surveillance of recipients' movements and of workers, offloads compliance burdens onto both, and intensifies tensions between the state, care workers and benefit recipients.

Why it matters for homecareOS: The most direct recent analysis of GPS + identity-verified check-in in home care. Warns that fraud-prevention framing masks real surveillance and administrative burden on carers and clients; relevant to homecareOS's GPS verification design, consent, data-minimisation and how location data on service users is captured and retained.

Gallopyn N, Iezzoni LI, 2020, Disability and Health Journal (Elsevier), 13(4):100938

In-depth interviews (21 personal-assistance consumers with significant disability and 20 workers) found EVV was widely seen as intrusive, reducing the flexibility of the consumer-worker relationship, and prone to technical difficulties, with fears it could harm recruitment and retention of workers - though it may help some consumers; authors urge policymakers to monitor its effects.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Captures the service-user and worker experience of mandated visit verification, including technical-failure and flexibility harms. Missed & short visits and care safety

homecareOS flags late/missed visits (red pulsing = overdue but window open; red solid = missed) and alerts the office and family.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
The Consequences of Unmet Needs for Assistance With Daily Life Activities Among Older Adults: A Systematic Review
Huang J, Qian X, Choi EPH, Chau PH, 2024, Medical Care Research and Review

Systematic review of 28 studies found that unmet needs for help with ADLs/IADLs were consistently associated with greater health-care utilisation (including hospitalisation and higher medical spending) and with adverse psychosocial outcomes such as anxiety and depression; evidence on falls and mortality was mixed.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Provides the core justification for the feature: when care that should be delivered is not, the downstream harms are real and measurable. Escalating late/missed visits before need goes unmet is aimed precisely at preventing the hospitalisation and distress this review documents.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Missed nursing care in community-based health care: A scoping review
Azzellino G, Vagnarelli P, Mengoli L, Aitella E, Passamonti M, Ginaldi L, De Martinis M, 2026, International Journal of Nursing Studies

Scoping review of 15 studies found that missed/omitted care is a widespread phenomenon across home, community and residential settings, most often affecting documentation, patient and family education, psychosocial support and preventive/rehabilitative activities, with staffing constraints, workload pressure, burnout and fragmented communication as the main drivers.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly evidences that care is routinely 'missed' in home/community settings and that time and staffing pressure are the drivers, exactly the failure mode a late/missed-visit flag and office/family alert is designed to detect and escalate.

Cohort / observational study
Adverse Consequences of Unmet Needs for Care in High-Need/High-Cost Older Adults
Beach SR, Schulz R, Friedman EM, Rodakowski J, Martsolf RG, James AE III, 2020, The Journals of Gerontology: Series B (Psychological Sciences and Social Sciences)

Analysis of 4,024 community-dwelling older adults with disability (NHATS 2011) found that unmet care needs produced concrete adverse consequences, with rates rising sharply with dependency: 21.4% for low-need adults versus 39.6% for those with probable dementia and 48.7% at end of life; high-need/high-cost status independently predicted more adverse consequences even after accounting for helper-network size.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Shows unmet care need translates into tangible harm and that risk is highest for the most dependent users (dementia, end-of-life). Supports prioritised, risk-weighted alerting so that a late or missed visit to a high-risk service user is escalated most urgently.

Cohort / observational study

Vlachantoni A, Evandrou M, Falkingham J, Qin M, 2024, Ageing & Society

Longitudinal analysis of older people in England (English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, waves 8-9) with difficulty bathing or dressing found unmet need is common and often persistent, with 27.8% having repeatedly unmet needs across the two waves and a further 5.3% became newly unmet; the study frames unmet need against documented higher risks of falls, physician and emergency-room visits, hospitalisation and mortality.

Why it matters for homecareOS: UK-specific evidence that unmet need among older people is common and frequently persistent, reinforcing the case for proactive visit monitoring; a repeatedly late or missed visit is a plausible mechanism by which unmet need becomes chronic rather than transient.

Why it matters for homecareOS: UK and home-care-specific evidence that simply having a commissioned care package does not guarantee needs are met, supporting the feature's premise that visit delivery (punctuality, completion, adequacy) must be actively monitored rather than assumed, and that under-delivery is the norm, not the exception.

Person-centred, outcomes-focused care planning

homecareOS digital care plans, goals and observations support person-centred, outcomes-focused care.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Personalised care planning for adults with chronic or long-term health conditions
Coulter A, Entwistle VA, Eccles A, Ryan S, Shepperd S, Perera R, 2015, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews

This Cochrane review of 19 RCTs (n=10,856) found personalised care planning produced small improvements in physical health (HbA1c MD -0.24%, systolic BP MD -2.64 mmHg), depression (SMD -0.36) and self-management self-efficacy (SMD 0.25), with larger effects when the process was comprehensive and integrated into routine care; effects on generic and condition-specific quality of life were not statistically significant.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Directly underpins the core homecareOS design premise: collaborative, documented care planning where the person sets goals and reviews progress. It supports claims of improved self-management and health outcomes while cautioning that QoL gains depend on planning being comprehensive and embedded in routine care rather than a one-off form.

Systematic review / meta-analysis
Complex interventions for improving independent living and quality of life amongst community-dwelling older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Ho L, Malden S, McGill K, Shimonovich M, Frost H, Aujla N, Ho IS-S, Shenkin SD, Hanratty B, Mercer SW, Guthrie B, 2023, Age and Ageing (Oxford Academic), Vol 52, Issue 7, afad132

In this meta-analysis of RCTs of community interventions for older adults (90% of which included individualised care planning and 94% holistic assessment), interventions modestly increased the likelihood of continuing to live at home (RR 1.05, moderate-quality evidence), reduced mortality by 14%, and improved cognition and instrumental activities of daily living; pooled quality-of-life scores did not significantly improve.

Why it matters for homecareOS: Shows that holistic assessment plus care planning, exactly the workflow homecareOS digitises for home care, is associated with better functional and independence outcomes for community-dwelling older adults, the platform's core population. Also a realistic benchmark: independence/ADL gains are more evident than measurable QoL change.

Randomised controlled trial
Enabling health and maintaining independence for older people at home: the 'HomeHealth' Randomised Controlled Trial
Walters K, et al., 2026, Health Technology Assessment, 30(43):1-21 (NIHR)

In this single-blind parallel RCT, an outcomes-focused home service in which support workers helped older people set and pursue personal goals (mobility, nutrition, socialising, psychological well-being) did not significantly improve the primary ADL measure (Barthel Index) at 12 months, but improved well-being, reduced psychological distress and frailty at 6 months, and cut unplanned hospital admissions by ~35% with high probability of cost-effectiveness. Why it matters for homecareOS: A recent UK RCT validating the person-centred, goal-setting model that homecareOS goals and observations are built around: structured collaborative goal-setting in the home improved well-being and reduced costly hospital use even where a narrow ADL primary endpoint was unchanged, evidence for framing benefits around well-being and service use, not only functional scores.

Cohort / observational study
Effects of a person-centred and health-promoting intervention in home care services - a non-randomized controlled trial
Lämås K, Bölenius K, Sandman P-O, Lindkvist M, Edvardsson D, 2021, BMC Geriatrics, 21:720

In a non-randomized controlled trial across five home care districts in northern Sweden (n=81 older adults), staff trained to hold health-promoting conversations and document individualised, shared-decision care plans helped the intervention group maintain 'thriving' while the control group declined (p=0.026); there were no significant effects on health-related quality of life or self-determination, and the intervention group reported some increase in negative emotions.

Why it matters for homecareOS: One of the few controlled trials set specifically in domiciliary/home care (homecareOS's exact setting) testing person-centred, individualised care plans with shared decision-making. It supports the person-centred documentation model for sustaining well-being ('thriving') but is a candid reminder that effects on formal QoL measures can be null, a useful caution for calibrated marketing claims.
