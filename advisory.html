The research behind the advice

My MBA asked whether home care can be made sustainable

My MBA at Wrexham University culminated in original research — not a literature essay, but a survey of home care agency owners across England, analysed for what actually drives a sustainable business. I framed it around the triple bottom line: profit, people and planet.

The headline finding surprised a lot of people:

Funding alone isn't the answer. The things that decide whether a home care agency survives are margins, staff pay, travel-time economics and long-term planning — and money poured in without fixing those leaks straight back out.

That's the difference between advice from someone who's read the sector and advice from someone who's measured it. I can talk to your numbers because I've studied the numbers.

What the research found

The levers that actually move a care business

The real margin is thinner than it looks

Local-authority rates look like a healthy gross margin — until mileage, pension, NI, training, recruitment, IT, insurance, PPE and regulatory cost eat it. Owners consistently told me the headline rate has to be meaningfully higher than most contracts pay to be genuinely sustainable.

Pay is the profit driver, not price

What an agency pays turned out to predict profitability better than what it charges. Cost discipline and a smart pay strategy matter more than chasing rate rises — and both are learnable.

Travel time is where money leaks

Most agencies don't properly cost travel time — and once unpaid travel is counted, effective hourly pay can quietly fall below the legal minimum. Fixing travel-time economics is one of the fastest wins available.

The VAT relief that isn't there

No VAT is charged on care itself — but agencies pay VAT on the overheads they need to deliver it (PPE, equipment, software, ICT, recruitment, utilities) and can't recover a penny. I argue care agencies should be able to claim relief on that input VAT, freeing cash to reinvest in pay and care.

The right client mix is a choice, not luck

A deliberate balance of private and local-authority clients — and knowing which to grow — is one of the clearest routes to a healthier, more resilient book of business.
