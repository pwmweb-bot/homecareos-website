Sponsor Licence Compliance for Home Care Agencies: The Plain-English Guide

If your agency employs carers under the Health and Care Worker visa, the sponsor licence duties are the highest-stakes admin you do. This guide explains record keeping, reporting, and right to work checks in plain English, what a Home Office compliance visit looks at, and where the licences actually get lost.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 25 August 2026
Guidance, not legal advice. This article summarises how sponsor licence compliance works for UK home care agencies in general terms. It is not immigration advice. Rules, thresholds and going rates change; always check current Home Office Sponsor Guidance on gov.uk, and take advice from a regulated immigration adviser or solicitor before acting on a specific case.

Why this is the admin that can end an agency

Since the Health and Care Worker visa opened to social care roles in February 2022, thousands of UK home care agencies have taken on a second regulator without always noticing. Alongside CQC, CIW, the Care Inspectorate or RQIA, a licensed sponsor answers to UK Visas and Immigration (UKVI), part of the Home Office. Unlike a care inspection, a UKVI compliance failure does not produce an action plan and a re-inspection date. It can produce a revoked licence, and with it the loss of every sponsored carer on the rota.

The uncomfortable part is that most licences are not lost to deliberate abuse. During the 2024-2025 compliance crackdown, home care agencies lost licences most commonly for inadequate record keeping, late or missing UKVI reports, or concerns about whether the sponsored vacancies were genuine. These are administrative failures, which means they are preventable, and mostly preventable with better systems rather than better intentions.

What does sponsor licence compliance mean for a home care agency? Home care agencies that sponsor carers under the Health and Care Worker visa carry five ongoing duties: right to work checks before employment starts and again at visa expiry, keeping sponsored-worker records (passport, visa, BRP or share code, contact details, attendance) accessible for audit, reporting changes to UKVI within 10 working days, maintaining genuine vacancies, and cooperating with Home Office compliance visits, which can be unannounced. Most licences are lost to record-keeping and reporting failures rather than deliberate breaches. Revocation gives each sponsored carer 60 days to find a new sponsor or leave the UK, and the agency a 5-year cooling-off period before it can apply again. Software such as homecareOS reduces the risk by making the evidence a by-product of normal operations: CoS tracking, right to work workflows, visa expiry alerts, and a staged queue of 10-day reporting triggers, all linked to the carer record and the rota.

The five sponsor licence duties in plain English

The Home Office Sponsor Guidance runs to hundreds of pages, but for a home care agency sponsoring under SOC codes 6135 (Senior Care Worker) and 6136 (Care Worker), five duties carry nearly all the weight:

  1. Right to work checks, done before employment starts and repeated at visa expiry, with evidence retained.
  2. Record keeping, holding each sponsored worker's passport, visa, BRP or share code, contact details and attendance in a form you can produce for a UKVI audit.
  3. Reporting duties, telling UKVI about changes to a sponsored worker's circumstances within 10 working days.
  4. Genuine vacancies, only sponsoring roles that reflect real care demand, with no over-sponsoring.
  5. Cooperation with compliance visits, which can arrive unannounced.

Everything below is one of these five, seen from a different angle.

Record keeping: what UKVI expects you to produce

The record-keeping duty is where the day-to-day work sits. For each sponsored carer, an auditor will expect the full chain: the Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS) reference, the role and salary it was assigned against, the visa and BRP or eVisa share code, English language proof, right to work evidence, current contact details, and attendance records that show the person is actually working the role they were sponsored for.

Two things make this harder than it sounds in a home care setting. First, the records change: carers move house, visas get renewed, share codes get re-issued, roles shift from Care Worker to Senior Care Worker. Static copies in a folder go stale without anyone noticing. Second, the attendance evidence is operational data. It lives in your rota and your visit records, not in the HR file, so a sponsor running visa admin on a spreadsheet has to reconcile two systems every time UKVI asks a question. That reconciliation gap is exactly where record-keeping findings come from.

Reporting duties: the 10 working day clock

UKVI requires sponsors to report most changes in a sponsored worker's circumstances within 10 working days, through the Sponsorship Management System (SMS), filed by your authorising officer or a Level 1 user. No software can file for you, and no software should claim to. The trigger events are the part agencies miss:

Trigger eventWhy it slips through
Address changeThe carer tells their coordinator, who updates the rota system. Nobody tells whoever holds the SMS login.
Role changeA promotion from Care Worker to Senior Care Worker is an HR event in the office and a reportable event to UKVI.
Salary changePay reviews and changed hours both count. A drop below the visa threshold is a compliance problem as well as a report.
Employment endingResignations and dismissals get processed through payroll; the UKVI report is a separate step that has its own deadline.
Prolonged absenceUnexplained non-attendance is reportable, but only visible if someone is watching attendance against the rota.

Notice the pattern: every trigger event is something your operational systems already know about before your compliance process does. The 10-day clock starts when the change happens, not when the office notices. That is why the practical fix is detection, wiring the events that already flow through the rota and the carer record into a queue someone is responsible for clearing.

Right to work checks: the statutory excuse

A compliant right to work check, done before employment starts and repeated at visa expiry, gives an employer a statutory excuse against a civil penalty if a worker later turns out not to have the right to work. The check itself follows the Home Office Right to Work Code of Practice: capture the share code, BRP or passport, verify it, and keep time-stamped evidence of when the check happened and who did it.

The failure mode for home care agencies is rarely the first check. It is the re-check. A carer hired in 2023 with a visa expiring in 2028 needs a diarised re-check five years after anyone last thought about it, usually after the person who did the original check has left. If your system does not carry the visa expiry date forward into someone's working day, the re-check simply does not happen, and the statutory excuse quietly lapses.

What a Home Office compliance visit looks at

Compliance visits can be unannounced, and they are evidence-led. In practice the auditor works through a documentation set that maps directly onto the duties above:

  • The list of sponsored workers, reconciled against your CoS log
  • Right to work evidence for each sponsored worker, initial checks and re-checks
  • Salary records, checked against the salary stated on each CoS and the visa threshold
  • Attendance records, showing the sponsored role is actually being worked
  • The change reports you have filed with UKVI, matched against what actually changed
  • Evidence that vacancies are genuine, that sponsorship reflects real care demand rather than ghost roles

On salary, the general Health and Care Worker minimum is the higher of £23,200 gross per year (as of April 2024) or the going rate for the SOC code, and the threshold has been rising, so current figures need checking on gov.uk before any CoS is issued. The genuineness question deserves particular attention because it is the hardest to evidence retrospectively: an auditor asking why you sponsored six carers last year wants to see the care demand those roles were filling, which for a domiciliary agency means rota and visit volume data, not a job advert.

Where software evidence changes the picture

None of the duties above can be delegated to software, but almost all of the evidence behind them can be generated by it. This is the design idea behind the homecareOS visa sponsorship module: the compliance trail should be a side-effect of normal operations, not a separate job. Concretely, that looks like:

  • CoS allocation tracking. Each Certificate of Sponsorship tracked from request through assignment, visa application, grant, expiry and renewal, linked to the carer record, the role, the salary and the supporting documents.
  • A right to work workflow. Share code, BRP or passport captured at onboarding, re-checked before visa expiry, time-stamped and stored against the carer record so every check produces a defensible statutory excuse.
  • Visa expiry alerts. Six-month, three-month and 30-day warnings per carer, surfaced to the registered manager and to the carer through the mobile app, so the re-check and the renewal both happen on time.
  • A staged 10-day reporting queue. The trigger events (address change, role change, salary change, employment ending, prolonged absence) detected from the systems that see them first, staged with the data needed to file through SMS, with an audit log of who did what and when.
  • Genuine vacancy evidence. CoS assignments linked to real visit demand from the rota, demonstrating that sponsorship is filling actual care need.
  • Salary threshold checks. Verification against the current minimum and the role's going rate, flagging if a sponsored carer's actual rota hours produce gross pay below threshold before it becomes a reporting issue.
  • A UKVI audit pack. One-click export of the documentation set a compliance visit typically asks for: sponsored worker list, CoS log, right to work evidence, salary records, attendance, and change reports filed.

The common thread is that each item reuses data the agency already generates by scheduling visits, paying carers and keeping the carer record current. The sponsor licence work stops being a parallel spreadsheet universe and becomes a view over the operational system, which is also why the audit pack can exist at all.

The failure points that actually cost licences

Pulling the threads together, the recurring reasons home care agencies lose licences are unglamorous:

  • Records that cannot be produced on the day. The documents exist somewhere, but not in a form an unannounced visit can be handed.
  • Reports filed late or not at all. Usually because the trigger event was invisible to the person holding the SMS login.
  • Genuineness-of-vacancy concerns. Sponsoring ahead of demand without being able to evidence the care hours behind the roles.
  • Pay drifting below threshold. Reduced hours quietly taking a sponsored carer's gross pay under the visa minimum.
  • Lapsed re-checks. Right to work evidence that was compliant at hiring and never refreshed at visa expiry.

The consequences are severe enough to state plainly, without dramatising them. Revocation means every sponsored carer typically has 60 days to find a new sponsor or leave the UK, and the agency faces a 5-year cooling-off period before it can apply for a new licence. Civil penalties for illegal working run to £45,000 per worker, rising to £60,000 for repeat offences, and the criminal offence of knowingly employing an illegal worker under Section 21 of the Immigration, Asylum and Nationality Act 2006 carries up to 5 years' imprisonment. The point of listing these is not fear; it is that every one of them sits downstream of the same administrative failures, and administrative failures respond to systems.

In summary

Sponsor licence compliance for a home care agency comes down to five duties, discharged continuously: check right to work, keep the records, report within 10 working days, sponsor genuine roles, and be ready for an unannounced visit. The agencies that hold their licences comfortably are not the ones with the thickest policy binders; they are the ones whose operational systems surface the trigger events and keep the evidence chain without anyone having to remember. Frequently asked questions

How long do sponsors have to report changes to UKVI?

Most changes must be reported within 10 working days: address change, role change, salary change, employment ending, prolonged absence. Reports go through the Sponsorship Management System (SMS), filed by the authorising officer or a Level 1 user. The clock starts when the change happens, not when the office finds out.

Are Home Office compliance visits announced in advance?

Not always. Compliance visits can be unannounced, and cooperating with them is a core sponsor duty. Auditors typically ask for the sponsored worker list, CoS log, right to work evidence, salary records, attendance data and the change reports filed with UKVI.

What records must a home care sponsor keep for each sponsored worker?

Passport, visa and BRP or eVisa share code, up-to-date contact details, attendance records, right to work evidence, the CoS reference linked to role and salary, and English language proof. Records must stay accessible for audit, and right to work checks must be repeated at visa expiry.

What happens if a home care agency loses its sponsor licence?

Sponsored workers become unable to continue working for the agency and typically have 60 days to find a new sponsor or leave the UK. The agency faces a 5-year cooling-off period before it can apply again. Most recent revocations in home care followed record-keeping or reporting failures rather than deliberate breaches.

Can software file UKVI reports on a sponsor's behalf?

No. The legal duty sits with the licence holder, and reporting is done through SMS by the authorising officer or a Level 1 user. What software can do is detect the trigger events, stage the report with the data needed to file it, and store the supporting evidence in audit-ready form, which is how homecareOS approaches it.
