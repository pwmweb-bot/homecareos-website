Ageing in Place: Why the Home Is Becoming the Primary Setting for Care

Across the United Kingdom, the setting in which care is delivered is quietly shifting. For most of the twentieth century, serious illness and frailty in later life led, sooner or later, to a hospital ward or a residential home. In the twenty-first, the destination is increasingly the person's own front room. This essay examines the demographic, policy and evidence forces pushing care into the home, and the formidable operational challenge of delivering it safely, at scale, across thousands of individual households.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 14 July 2026

The demographic tide

The starting point for any serious discussion of the future of care is arithmetic. The United Kingdom is ageing, and the fastest growth is at the very oldest end of the age range, precisely the group most likely to need help with daily living. The population aged 85 and over is projected to roughly double, from around 1.75 million (2.5 per cent of the population) in mid-2024 to about 3.6 million (4.9 per cent) by mid-2049, while the pensionable-age population is set to reach roughly one in five people by the mid-2030s (ONS, 2026).

This is not a peculiarly British phenomenon. The World Health Organization projects that the number of people worldwide aged 60 and over will rise from one billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion by 2030 and 2.1 billion by 2050, with the share of the population in that age band climbing from 12 to 22 per cent between 2015 and 2050; the population aged 80 and over is expected to triple over the same period, reaching some 426 million (WHO, 2025). Ageing populations do not merely live longer; they more often live with several long-term conditions simultaneously, which complicates and intensifies the care they require (WHO, 2025).

The implication is straightforward but heavy. Demand for care is growing structurally, not cyclically, and it is concentrated in the population least able to travel to receive it. Any system designed around the assumption that care happens principally in institutions is running against the demographic grain.

What older people actually want

Demography sets the scale of the challenge; preference points to the answer. The evidence on where older people wish to receive care is unusually consistent. A systematic review across OECD countries found that "an overwhelming majority of older adults wish to age in place as it fosters a sense of identity, autonomy and connectedness" (Bolster-Foucault et al., 2024). Remaining at home is not simply the cheaper or more convenient option; for many people it is bound up with who they are: their routines, their neighbourhood, their memories, their sense of control over their own lives.

That preference should be treated with care rather than romanticised. The same review is clear that who actually manages to age in place is shaped by systematic social inequities: income, education, housing quality, ethnicity and gender all influence whether the wish becomes the reality (Bolster-Foucault et al., 2024). A policy of "home first" that ignores these gradients risks delivering ageing in place comfortably for the well-housed and well-supported, while leaving others in homes that are unsafe, unadapted or isolating. The preference is near-universal; the capacity to realise it is not.

The home as the new hospital

Policy and clinical practice have begun to follow both the demographics and the preference. In England, the direction of travel long predates the current decade: analysis of adult social care noted that provision had already shifted "towards providing care at home rather than in residential settings", and that the single most common reason for a delayed discharge from hospital was a person waiting for a care package in their own home, even as post-discharge reablement, the short-term support that helps people regain independence, reached only a small minority of those leaving hospital (Morris, 2019).

The 2025 ten-year plan for the NHS in England makes the ambition explicit. It sets out three "radical shifts" (from hospital to community, from analogue to digital, and from sickness to prevention) and describes a "neighbourhood NHS" that moves care closer to, and increasingly into, people's homes (DHSC, 2025). The language matters: the home is being reframed not as the place people go when treatment ends, but as a legitimate setting for treatment itself.

Nowhere is this clearer than in the rapid expansion of virtual wards, also known as hospital at home. These deliver acute, hospital-level care at a patient's "usual place of residence" through step-up models (avoiding an admission) and step-down models (enabling an earlier discharge), with services expected to run at high occupancy; NHS England estimates that roughly 2.5 virtual-ward admissions are associated with one fewer non-elective hospital admission (NHS England, 2024). Care that once required a bed on a ward is being reconstituted around a bed in a bedroom.

The evidence for this model is genuinely promising but should be reported honestly, including its limits. A Cochrane review of admission-avoidance hospital at home, drawing on twenty randomised trials and around 3,100 participants, concluded that the approach probably makes little or no difference to mortality, health status or hospital readmission. Its clearer benefits lie elsewhere: it probably reduces the likelihood of a person moving into residential care at six months, may improve patient satisfaction, and probably decreases costs (Edgar et al., 2024). In other words, hospital at home is not usually a route to better clinical outcomes than a ward, but it can help people stay in their own homes, at lower cost, without doing them harm. For a system committed to ageing in place, that is a substantial prize.

The operational challenge nobody should underestimate

If the home is to carry more of the care that hospitals and residential settings once absorbed, someone has to deliver it, reliably, safely and at enormous scale. This is where ambition meets friction. Domiciliary (home) care is already the single largest part of the adult social care workforce in England, accounting for around 46 per cent of filled posts, or roughly 740,000 people. It also carries the highest vacancy rate of any service type, at 9.7 per cent, against a sector-wide vacancy rate of 7.0 per cent and some 111,000 vacancies overall (Skills for Care, 2025). The part of the system being asked to grow fastest is the part that already finds it hardest to recruit.

The consequences of that squeeze are visible in unmet need. Around two million people aged 65 and over in England have some unmet need for social care, a figure that rises to more than one in four of those aged over 80, including, for example, over a million people who struggle to dress and hundreds of thousands who struggle to bathe without help (Age UK, 2024). Moving care into the home does not, by itself, close that gap; done badly, it can widen it, because a missed visit in a private home is far less visible than an empty chair on a ward.

There is a technological dependency, too. Roughly 1.8 million people in the UK rely on telecare, the alarms and sensors that summon help when an older person falls or takes ill at home. The migration of the telephone network from analogue to digital, originally slated for 2025 and now extended towards 2027 after a series of safety incidents, threatens call failures and loss of function during power cuts unless devices have battery backup (Hamblin & Whitfield, 2025). The infrastructure that makes independent living safe is itself in transition, a theme explored further in our analysis of technology-enabled care and the analogue switch-off. Ageing in place, then, is not a soft option. It asks a stretched workforce, supported by ageing infrastructure, to deliver increasingly complex care across tens of thousands of front doors, and to prove, visit by visit, that it was delivered.

Where homecareOS fits: coordination infrastructure for care at home

This is the practical problem homecareOS was built to address. If the home is becoming the primary setting for care, the binding constraint is no longer the willingness to provide it but the ability to coordinate it: to know who is going where, that they arrived, what they did, and whether anything went wrong, across a workforce that is mobile, part-time and often working in areas with poor mobile signal. Coordination at that scale is an information problem, and information problems are solved with the right operating system.

homecareOS treats the visit as the root of everything, so a double-up call, a shadowing trainee or an unannounced spot check are all handled within a single, auditable model rather than bolted on as exceptions. Its visit-centric scheduling gives coordinators a single screen for every carer and every hour of the day, with an AI cover finder that ranks available carers on distance, continuity, hours headroom and preference when someone calls in sick at six in the morning, the moment at which ageing in place most often fails in practice. The carer's mobile app works offline, capturing notes and medication records on a no-signal visit and syncing them when connectivity returns, so the record survives the basement flat and the rural lane.

The same architecture supports the two things regulators and families most want from care at home: a trustworthy record and genuine visibility. Person-centred digital care records are immutable and timestamped, feeding compliance dashboards in real time rather than in the panicked week before an inspection, while a family portal turns anxious phone calls into instant confirmations that a parent's visit happened. Crucially, that compliance layer is built for all four UK nations from the outset (CQC in England, CIW in Wales, the Care Inspectorate in Scotland and RQIA in Northern Ireland) because ageing in place is a devolved reality with four different rulebooks, not one. The pricing follows the same logic of matching cost to operational reality: agencies are billed per active carer, so the office team that does the coordinating is never metered.

The risks worth naming

An honest account of ageing in place must sit with its trade-offs rather than wave them away. Three deserve emphasis. The first is isolation. The same connectedness that makes the home the preferred setting can, for a housebound person seeing only a rota of carers, tip into loneliness; the home can be a place of belonging or a place of confinement, and which one it becomes depends heavily on the surrounding support (Bolster-Foucault et al., 2024). Technology that coordinates visits does not, on its own, address the human need behind them.

The second is carer capacity. A model that shifts more complex care into the home depends on a workforce that is already the hardest to recruit and retain, and no scheduling system can conjure staff who are not there (Skills for Care, 2025). Better tools can make each carer's time go further and each agency more able to hold on to the people it has, but they are not a substitute for pay, status and a serious workforce strategy, the subject of our companion essay on the future of the care workforce.

The third is unmet need and equity. If ageing in place is delivered well only for those with good housing, informal support and the confidence to navigate the system, it will deepen the very inequities the evidence already documents (Bolster-Foucault et al., 2024; Age UK, 2024). The measure of success is not how many people are kept out of hospital, but whether the two million older people with unmet need today are more or fewer tomorrow.

The bottom line

The direction is not seriously in doubt. Demography is pushing demand upward and concentrating it among the oldest; older people themselves overwhelmingly want to remain at home; and policy and clinical practice are steadily reconstituting the home as a setting for treatment as well as support (ONS, 2026; Bolster-Foucault et al., 2024; DHSC, 2025). The open question is not whether the home becomes the primary setting for care but how well we manage the transition: whether it is delivered safely and equitably, or unevenly and out of sight.

That is ultimately a question of coordination: of turning the intention to care for people at home into thousands of visits that reliably happen, are properly recorded, and are visible to the families and regulators who depend on them. The demographic wave is arriving regardless. The task for the sector, and for the software that supports it, is to be ready for it.
