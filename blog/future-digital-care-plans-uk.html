The Future of Digital Care Plans in the UK

Digital care plans are transforming how agencies deliver person-centred care. We explore the trends shaping the industry (DSCR standards, GP Connect, outcome modelling) and what providers should prepare for in 2026 and beyond.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 24 February 2026

From paper to point-of-care

The UK home care sector is mid-transition. NHS England's DSCR (Digital Social Care Records) programme has been pushing the sector from paper to digital since 2022. CQC's Single Assessment Framework rewards demonstrable evidence over written-up paperwork. Local authority frameworks increasingly require DSCR-listed providers. The direction of travel is settled. The interesting question is what comes after.

"Digital care plan" used to mean "we typed the paper care plan into a Word document and saved it on the office network". That's not a digital care plan. It's a digital filing cabinet. A real digital care plan is an active object: it's accessible at the point of care (on a carer's phone), structured (so changes can be queried and audited), connected (linked to the rest of the care record), and continuously updated (so the plan and the reality stay in sync).

This piece looks at where things are going, not where they are now. Five trends that will shape the next 2-3 years.

1. DSCR standardisation will keep tightening

NHS England's DSCR Standards (formerly SCRA) define what assured digital care record software must do. The standards are still maturing. Early versions focused on basic data capture, but the 2026 expansion is starting to demand interoperability (data leaving your platform in standardised formats), audit trail integrity (no silent edits), and access auditing (you can prove who saw what and when).

For agencies choosing software now, the question is "is this supplier DSCR-listed AND keeping pace with the evolving standard?" Not just "are they listed today?" A platform listed in 2024 that hasn't shipped material updates against later versions of the standard won't stay listed.

2. GP Connect adoption will become routine

GP Connect is the NHS service that lets approved care providers (with the service user's consent) see relevant parts of the GP record: current medications, allergies, key conditions, recent appointments. For home care specifically, this matters because medication errors are the most common safeguarding incident category, and the most common cause of medication errors is drift between what the carer believes the prescription is and what the GP has actually prescribed.

GP Connect adoption among home care providers is currently low but rising. By 2027 we expect it to be considered table stakes. Agencies without it will look like they're skipping a basic safety check. Software platforms need to build the integration; agencies need to build the consent process. Both take time.

3. Outcome modelling will replace task lists

Most current care plans are essentially structured task lists: "support with personal care", "prompt medication", "prepare lunch". That's useful but limited. It tells a carer what to do, not what success looks like.

The shift now happening is towards outcome modelling: alongside tasks, plans capture goals ("maintain independent toileting"), preferences ("prefers showering in the evening"), and outcome measures ("hospital admission avoided", "weight stable", "social engagement maintained"). Carers can record observations against outcomes, not just against tasks. Over time this gives families, GPs, and CQC a much richer picture than "tasks completed: 100%".

This isn't bureaucracy added on top. Done well, it's a richer way to capture what good care actually is. Agencies that adopt outcome modelling tend to score better on CQC's Caring and Responsive key questions.

4. Point-of-care visibility will replace office-bound plans

The biggest design failure in legacy home care software is plans that exist in the office. The carer arriving at 7:15am needs to know in the next 30 seconds: who is this person, what are their preferences, what do I do first, what should I be alert to. If that information is buried three taps deep in a complex interface (or worse, only available on the office desktop), it isn't useful.

Modern digital care plans surface the right information at the right time. Open the visit on the carer app, see a one-screen summary: name, photo with consent, preferences, today's tasks, current medications with confirmation tap-targets, recent observations from the previous carer, and one-tap access to the full plan when needed. Everything else is available, but not in the way of doing the work.

Agencies switching from office-bound plans to point-of-care plans report fewer "the carer didn't know about X" complaints, faster onboarding for new carers, and improved continuity feedback from service users.

5. AI augmentation, selectively useful, often overhyped

"AI care plans" is becoming a marketing phrase. The honest assessment of where AI genuinely helps with care plans in 2026:

  • Helpful: Drafting a first-pass plan from an assessment document, surfacing inconsistencies between the plan and recent visit notes, flagging missing elements (e.g. "no pain management plan despite 3 mentions of pain in last 7 visits").
  • Helpful but careful: Suggesting outcome measures based on care needs ("for someone with this profile, you might track these outcomes"). Useful as a prompt, dangerous as an automatic decision.
  • Not yet useful: Generating personalised care plans without human review. The risk of inappropriate suggestions makes this category strictly off-limits.

The pattern: AI is a useful drafting and consistency tool, not a replacement for clinical or care judgement. Anything more than that is a marketing claim, not a feature.

A clarifier: domiciliary vs residential

This piece is about home care (domiciliary) digital care plans. Residential care platforms like Person Centred Software and Nourish have made huge progress on digital care plans for care homes. The user research and design context is quite different from home care, where the carer arrives alone for 30 minutes, often without a colleague to ask. Same family of problems, different shape.

What agencies should do now

If you're planning a software change in 2026, three things to evaluate honestly:

  1. Are the digital care plans actually used at the point of care? Watch a carer for an hour. If they look at the plan once at the start of the visit and never again, the design is wrong. If they reference it naturally throughout, for preferences, for current medications, for observation prompts, the design is working.
  2. How are plans updated? A plan that requires office staff to update it stays out of date. A plan that carers can suggest updates to (with manager approval) stays current.
  3. Is the supplier DSCR-listed and keeping pace? Check both. Don't just ask "are you listed?" Ask "what's your DSCR roadmap for the next 12 months?"

Frequently asked questions

What is a digital care plan?

A digital care plan is a structured, software-managed record of a person's care needs, goals, preferences, risks, and the way care is delivered. Unlike a paper plan, it's updated continuously, accessible from a phone, and connected to the rest of the care record so changes propagate automatically.

What is DSCR (Digital Social Care Records)?

An NHS England programme driving adoption of digital care records across adult social care. The programme funds providers to move from paper to digital, lists assured suppliers (DSCR-listed), and works with CQC and ICBs on standards.

What is GP Connect?

An NHS service that lets approved care providers (with consent) see a service user's GP record, current medications, allergies, recent appointments. For home care, the most useful application is catching medication-record drift before it causes a safeguarding incident.

What's the difference between a care plan and care notes?

Care notes describe what happened during a visit. The care plan describes what should happen and why. The plan is the standing intent; notes are the running log. A good digital care record connects them.

Are digital care plans mandatory in the UK?

Not legally mandatory yet, but increasingly expected. CQC expects structured plans, NHS England's DSCR programme is steadily moving the sector to digital, and most ICBs and local authority frameworks now require DSCR-listed providers for new contracts.
