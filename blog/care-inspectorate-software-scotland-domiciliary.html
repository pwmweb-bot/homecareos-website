Care Inspectorate Software: Everything Scottish Domiciliary Agencies Need to Know

Scotland has its own regulator, its own standards, its own workforce register and its own background-checking scheme. A platform configured only for CQC in England will make your inspections harder, not easier. Here's what care-at-home agencies in Scotland are actually accountable for in 2026, and exactly what your software should be doing to evidence it.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 24 June 2026

Most home care software content on the internet is written for England: CQC, the Single Assessment Framework, the Care Act, DBS checks. Almost none of it applies north of the border. If you run a care-at-home service in Aberdeen, Glasgow, Inverness or Dumfries, the rules you're inspected against are set by a different regulator, backed by different legislation, and evidenced against a different framework. Our CQC compliance guide is useful background, but treat it as a description of the English system. CQC, the Care Act 2014 and the Single Assessment Framework do not apply in Scotland at all.

This is the Scottish equivalent: who regulates you, what you're graded on, how your workforce is registered, and (the practical bit) what a good Care Inspectorate software setup does to make it all evidence itself as a by-product of daily work.

The Care Inspectorate is your regulator

Care at home and housing support services in Scotland are registered with and inspected by the Care Inspectorate (Social Care and Social Work Improvement Scotland). It is the direct counterpart to CQC in England, CIW in Wales and RQIA in Northern Ireland, but it is its own body, with its own methodology and its own published grades.

The Care Inspectorate registers your service, sets conditions of registration, inspects on a risk-based schedule, publishes an inspection report with grades, and can issue requirements, recommendations or formal enforcement (including improvement notices) where a service falls short. Inspections of care at home are increasingly intelligence-led: your grades history, complaints, notifications and annual return all feed the decision about when and how closely you're next inspected.

The Health and Social Care Standards

Scottish services are inspected against the Health and Social Care Standards ("My support, my life"), which set out what people should expect from any care or support in Scotland. They're built around five overarching principles (dignity and respect, compassion, being included, responsive care and support, and wellbeing) and are written from the person's point of view rather than as a checklist for providers.

That "I" framing matters for your records. Inspectors want to see that a person's own outcomes, choices and wishes drive their care plan, not a generic template. Person-led care planning software, where each service user's plan is genuinely their own narrative rather than a form, is what makes Standards-aligned evidence natural rather than retrofitted.

The quality framework, key questions and six-point grades

The Care Inspectorate assesses services using a quality framework structured around a small number of key questions. For care at home, these are typically:

  • How well do we support people's wellbeing?
  • How good is our leadership?
  • How good is our staff team?
  • How good is our setting? (where relevant)
  • How well is our care and support planned?

Not every key question is graded at every inspection. Inspectors commonly focus on wellbeing (Key Question 1) and care planning (Key Question 5), grading others as the picture requires. Each graded key question is scored on the Care Inspectorate's six-point scale:

GradeMeaningWhat it signals
6ExcellentSector-leading, outstanding practice
5Very GoodMajor strengths, minor areas to improve
4GoodImportant strengths outweigh areas for improvement
3AdequateStrengths just outweigh weaknesses; improvement needed
2WeakSignificant weaknesses; requirements likely
1UnsatisfactorySerious concerns; enforcement likely

This is a genuinely different scale from CQC's four-word ratings (Outstanding / Good / Requires Improvement / Inadequate). Any dashboard or self-evaluation tooling that only speaks in CQC ratings is mapping your evidence to the wrong scoring system. Your compliance software should present self-evaluation against the key questions and the 1-6 grades, the language your inspector actually uses.

SSSC registration and PRTL: the workforce piece

This is the single biggest divergence from England. In Scotland, the care workforce is individually regulated. Care at home and housing support workers must register with the Scottish Social Services Council (SSSC), and registration carries real conditions:

  • Qualifications: registrants hold, or work towards within a set period, the relevant qualification for their role, usually an SVQ. A worker who doesn't achieve it in time can be removed from the register, which means they can no longer legally do the work.
  • Post Registration Training and Learning (PRTL): every registrant must complete and record a minimum amount of continuous learning across each registration period, and be able to produce evidence of it.
  • Codes of Practice: registrants must work to the SSSC Codes, and employers to the employer Code.

Practically, that means your software needs to track, per carer: the SSSC registration number, the part of the register they're on, the registration expiry/renewal date, the qualification they hold or are working towards with its own deadline, and their PRTL progress. Training-expiry alerts that only think in terms of mandatory course refreshers miss half the picture in Scotland. The register itself is a hard deadline. A worker whose SSSC registration lapses is a compliance and staffing problem at the same time.

PVG, not DBS

Background checking in Scotland runs through the Protecting Vulnerable Groups (PVG) scheme, operated by Disclosure Scotland, not the DBS, which is the England-and-Wales system. Anyone doing "regulated work" with protected adults (or children) should be a PVG scheme member.

The record you keep is different in shape from a DBS certificate: a PVG scheme membership number, the date of the disclosure, and your process for keeping it current. Software built only for England will offer you a "DBS number" field and a DBS expiry reminder, the wrong data type for a Scottish agency. What you need are dedicated PVG fields and re-check prompts, so recruitment and ongoing compliance both hold the right evidence.

Annual return and notifications

Two ongoing obligations catch out agencies that treat compliance as an inspection-day event:

  • The annual return: every registered service submits a yearly return to the Care Inspectorate covering staffing (including SSSC registration status), hours of care delivered, complaints and other operational data. If those figures live in spreadsheets and memory, the return is a scramble. If they live in one platform, it's an export.
  • Notifications: you must notify the Care Inspectorate when certain events occur, for example the death of a person using the service, serious incidents or injuries, allegations of abuse, an outbreak of infection, or a significant change to the service. Each has expected timescales.

Notifications are exactly the kind of thing a good incident workflow should catch: log the event, flag that it is notifiable, record when and how the Care Inspectorate was told, and keep the audit trail. Doing this by email and hoping you remember is how notifications get missed, and a missed notification is itself a finding.

What good Care Inspectorate software actually does

Pulling the threads together, here's the practical specification for home care software in Scotland that stands up to inspection rather than fighting it:

  • Self-evaluation aligned to the Standards and quality framework: evidence organised by key question, graded on the 1-6 scale, mapped to the Health and Social Care Standards, not force-fitted to CQC ratings.
  • SSSC-aware workforce records: registration numbers, register part, renewal dates, qualification deadlines and PRTL tracking, with alerts that treat a lapsing registration as urgent.
  • PVG fields, not DBS fields: scheme membership numbers and re-check prompts as first-class data.
  • Notification logging: incidents flagged as notifiable, with the date and method of notifying the Care Inspectorate recorded and audit-trailed.
  • Annual-return-ready reporting: staffing, hours of care, and complaints data you can export rather than reconstruct.
  • Person-led care planning: plans written from the individual's outcomes, which is what the Standards expect to see.

Choosing a platform as a Scottish agency

When you shortlist software, the four-nation question is your very first cut. Ask any vendor to show you a worked self-evaluation and a carer compliance record for a Scottish service, with SSSC and PVG on screen, graded on the six-point scale. If all they can produce is a CQC dashboard, they aren't configured for you, whatever the sales deck says. Our 12-point checklist for choosing home care software puts regulatory fit near the top for exactly this reason, and the broader UK home care software guide covers how the main platforms handle (or don't handle) the devolved nations.

Scotland's regime isn't harder than England's. It's just genuinely different, and the difference is in the details that inspections turn on. Get software that speaks Scottish from day one and the compliance overhead largely disappears into your normal daily work.
