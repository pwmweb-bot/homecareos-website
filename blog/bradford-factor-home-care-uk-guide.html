Bradford Factor in Home Care: The Definitive UK Guide

A small formula with a big footprint in home care HR. Used carefully, the Bradford Factor catches absence patterns early. Used badly, it punishes people for being unwell. Here's the practical, ethical guide for UK domiciliary agencies — what it is, how it works, where it earns its keep, and what to watch out for.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 7 May 2026

Why home care agencies cannot ignore absence patterns

If a carer in a residential setting calls in sick, a colleague picks up the slack down the corridor. If a domiciliary carer calls in sick at 6am, ten service users wake up to a stranger on the doorstep — or worse, no carer at all. The economic cost of an absent home care worker is not the day's wages saved. It is the cancelled visit, the missed medication, the angry family WhatsApp, the council Key Performance Indicator dropping below 95%, and the 90 minutes your coordinator spends on the phone re-shuffling the rota.

That asymmetry is why home care has always cared more about absence than other sectors. And it is why the Bradford Factor — a formula developed at Bradford University School of Management in the 1980s and now widely cited in CIPD absence-management guidance and ACAS sickness-absence policy frameworks — has quietly become one of the most-used HR tools in UK home care.

This guide covers the formula, the thresholds, real-world examples, the legal and ethical landmines, and how it actually works inside homecareOS staff management. It is the only Bradford Factor guide written specifically for UK domiciliary agencies. If you are a registered manager, coordinator, or owner trying to decide whether to use this tool — and how — start here.

What is the Bradford Factor?

The Bradford Factor is a single number that scores each employee's absence pattern over a rolling window — typically the previous 52 weeks. The higher the number, the more disruptive the absence pattern is judged to be. The formula is brutally simple:

B = S² × D

Bradford score = (absence spells)² × (total days absent)

S is the number of separate absence spells. D is the total days absent. Both measured over the same 52-week rolling window.

The squared term on S is the whole point. It says: frequency is more disruptive than duration. One person who has flu for ten consecutive days scores 10 (1² × 10). Another person who calls in sick on five separate single days scores 125 (5² × 5). The first person was sicker. The second person scored 12.5× higher. This is by design.

For home care specifically, this is the right shape. Ten consecutive days off can be planned around — you book cover for the run, the service users meet a temporary regular face, life goes on. Five separate single days, scattered randomly, is the operational nightmare: you scramble cover five separate times, each time at short notice, with five separate continuity disruptions for service users.

RAG thresholds — what counts as a "high" score?

Most UK employers using the Bradford Factor adopt a red/amber/green banding:

Band Score Typical action
Green0–99Within normal range. No action.
Amber100–199Informal welfare conversation. Check whether anything is going on the carer might want help with.
Red200+Formal review under your contractual sickness absence policy.

These thresholds are guidance, not law. ACAS does not publish standard Bradford thresholds, and there is no statutory definition. Some agencies set the amber threshold at 50 (very tight); others at 150 (loose). What matters is that the threshold is documented in your sickness absence policy, communicated to staff, and applied consistently.

A useful rule of thumb: an amber threshold of 100 is hit by either four 1-day absences (4² × 4 = 64 — so still green) or five 1-day absences (5² × 5 = 125 — amber). If five short absences in a year feels like the right point for a conversation, 100 is your number.

Three real-world carer scenarios

To make the formula concrete, here are three scenarios drawn from typical UK home care patterns:

Example 1: Reliable carer with one bout of flu

Janet has worked at the agency for 4 years with no sickness absence. In January she gets influenza B and is off for 8 consecutive days.

  • S (separate spells) = 1
  • D (total days) = 8
  • Bradford score = 1² × 8 = 8 (green)

Janet's score is well within tolerance. The flu was bad luck. No follow-up beyond a return-to-work conversation.

Example 2: The pattern of Monday absences

Liam has been off six times in the last 12 months — five times for one day each (always a Monday), once for three days (a stomach bug).

  • S = 6
  • D = 5 + 3 = 8
  • Bradford score = 6² × 8 = 288 (red)

Same number of total days as Janet. Score 36× higher. The Monday pattern is the signal — that should prompt a conversation, not a punishment. Maybe Liam has a chronic condition flaring at the start of each week, maybe he is struggling with weekend caring responsibilities, maybe he hates Mondays. The Bradford score doesn't tell you why; it tells you something is consistent enough to warrant noticing.

Example 3: The person managing a chronic condition

Sade has rheumatoid arthritis. Her flares are unpredictable but documented as a disability under the Equality Act 2010. Over 12 months she has 4 flare-related absences totalling 9 days.

  • If included: 4² × 9 = 144 (amber) — this would be a problem
  • If RA-related absences are correctly excluded: 0 (green)

This is the most important worked example in this guide. Disability-related absence should usually be excluded from the Bradford calculation as a reasonable adjustment. Failing to do so is both ethically wrong and a probable Equality Act 2010 breach. Any home care platform that calculates the Bradford Factor automatically must let you tag absences with reason codes and exclude specific codes from the score. homecareOS does this; not all competitors do.

The legal and ethical landmines

The Bradford Factor itself is legal in the UK. How you use it can quickly become illegal or unfair. Five things to be careful about:

  1. Exclude protected absence. Disability-related, pregnancy-related, and bereavement absence should generally not feed the score. The Equality Act 2010 places a duty on employers to make reasonable adjustments — applying the same trigger threshold to a disabled employee as to a non-disabled one is the textbook example of failing that duty.
  2. The score is a flag, not a verdict. It says "look here", not "fire this person". ACAS guidance on sickness absence is clear: absence-management decisions should be based on the individual circumstances, not a number.
  3. Communicate the policy. If carers don't know the thresholds, the score, or the consequences, you have a procedural fairness problem at the first formal review. Put thresholds in your sickness policy, in your handbook, and on every carer profile in your software.
  4. Apply consistently. "We use the Bradford Factor for the field carers but not the office team" creates an indirect discrimination risk if the field carer demographic differs materially from the office demographic. If you use it, use it everywhere.
  5. Don't rely on it alone. A reliable, recently-bereaved carer with three short absences in a row may score 100 in a week. A perpetual absconder who takes one 200-day stretch will score 200. The first person needs support; the second person is a serious operational problem. The score will not always tell you which is which.

For agencies with formal HR support, treat this as a starter list and check your Bradford Factor implementation against your sickness absence policy and any Equality Act risk assessment. For smaller agencies without dedicated HR, ACAS publishes free guidance on managing sickness absence at acas.org.uk — start there before you adopt any threshold.

Bradford Factor vs other absence metrics

Some agencies combine Bradford with other metrics. Worth knowing what each one tells you:

Metric What it measures Best for
Bradford FactorFrequency of absence (squared) × days lostSpotting fragmented absence patterns
Lost-time rateDays absent ÷ days scheduled, as %High-level workforce planning
Absence frequencyNumber of absence spells per periodSame signal as Bradford but without the squared weight
Average durationTotal days ÷ number of spellsDistinguishing short-term sickness from long-term illness
Return-to-work completion% of absences with completed RTW interviewCompliance auditing

None of these is sufficient on its own. Bradford gives you the early warning; the others give you the context. A good home care management platform tracks all five.

Should your agency actually use it?

Honest answer: it depends on your size and your management culture.

Use it if you have more than 20 carers, you have a documented sickness absence policy, your managers are trained to have welfare conversations, and you can commit to excluding protected absences correctly. Used this way, it's a useful early-warning system that catches real issues — pattern-of-Monday absences, undiagnosed conditions, financial or caring stress — earlier than you'd otherwise notice.

Think twice if you are a 5-carer micro-agency where the manager already knows everyone's life situation, or if you don't yet have a formal absence policy. In those settings, a number on a screen adds bureaucracy without insight.

Don't use it if your only plan is to apply mechanical disciplinary triggers without conversation. The Bradford Factor used that way is, in ACAS's diplomatic phrasing, "associated with employment tribunal claims".

Implementation checklist

If you decide to introduce the Bradford Factor at your agency, here is the minimum you should do before turning it on:

  1. Update your sickness absence policy to reference the Bradford Factor and your specific thresholds. Get sign-off from HR / employment law adviser.
  2. Decide your reason-code exclusion list (the homecareOS default is a reasonable starting point: disability-related, pregnancy/maternity, bereavement, jury service, dependants leave).
  3. Communicate the policy in writing to all staff. Run a 30-minute training session for managers on what the score does and does not mean.
  4. Make the score visible to carers themselves — not just management. Transparency reduces the "secret HR file" feeling.
  5. Set up a calendar reminder for a 6-month review of the thresholds. The first set you choose will probably be wrong; that's fine if you commit to revising them.
  6. Decide upfront what an "amber conversation" sounds like. It is not a disciplinary; it is a check-in. Train your managers in the difference.

Bringing it together

The Bradford Factor is one of the most under-used and most-misused tools in UK home care HR. Most agencies don't track it because their software won't calculate it. Of those that do, a meaningful minority use it as a disciplinary cudgel, which is both ethically wrong and operationally counterproductive — your best carers will leave faster than your worst.

Used correctly, it gives you a 52-week absence-pattern X-ray for every carer, with the operational signal — frequency over duration — that matters most for domiciliary work. Combined with proper exclusions, communicated thresholds, and a culture where amber means "let's talk" rather than "you're in trouble", it is one of the most useful retention tools you can deploy.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Bradford Factor formula?

The Bradford Factor is calculated as S² × D, where S is the number of separate absence spells over a fixed window (usually 52 weeks) and D is the total days absent in that window. So three separate two-day absences (3² × 6 = 54) score higher than one six-day absence (1² × 6 = 6) — the formula deliberately weights frequency over duration.

What are the typical Bradford Factor thresholds?

Most UK employers use a RAG (red/amber/green) banding: 0–99 green (acceptable), 100–199 amber (informal review), 200+ red (formal review). These are guidance thresholds, not legal limits, and home care agencies should set their own based on operational tolerance.

Is the Bradford Factor legal in the UK?

Yes — the Bradford Factor itself is a measurement tool, not a disciplinary procedure. It is legal to use in the UK. What matters is how you act on the score: any absence-management action must follow ACAS guidance, your contractual sickness procedure, and Equality Act 2010 reasonable-adjustment duties (so disability- or pregnancy-related absences should typically be excluded from the score).

Should home care agencies use the Bradford Factor?

Used carefully, yes. Home care has high absence sensitivity (one missed carer means a missed visit), so spotting absence-pattern changes early is genuinely valuable. The danger is using a single number to make decisions about people. Treat the Bradford Factor as a flag for a conversation, not a verdict.

Does homecareOS calculate the Bradford Factor automatically?

The score is shown with RAG banding on every carer profile, and managers receive a daily digest of carers crossing thresholds. No competitor home care platform calculates this natively.

What absences should be excluded from the Bradford Factor?

ACAS guidance and most legal advice recommends excluding: disability-related absence (Equality Act 2010), pregnancy and maternity-related absence, bereavement leave, jury service, and any pre-agreed reasonable adjustments. homecareOS lets you tag absences with a reason code and exclude specific reason codes from the calculation.
