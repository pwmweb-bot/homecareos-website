Fewer Miles, More Care: Turning Scattered Visits Into Efficient Runs

A home care day built as a scatter of separate trips is a day half-spent behind the wheel. Visit optimisation is the craft of turning that scatter into tight, sensible runs, and handing each run to the carer best suited to it. Done well, it gives hours back to care.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 6 September 2026

Ask any home care coordinator where the day leaks time, and the answer is rarely the visits themselves. It is the gaps between them. A carer sent from one side of town to the other and back again spends a large slice of a paid shift driving, not caring, and every extra mile is a mile that could have been a cup of tea and a proper conversation with the person at the next door.

Visit optimisation is the discipline of closing those gaps. It is not about rushing carers or squeezing calls together until they are unsafe. It is about arranging the same work in a smarter order, so a carer works one patch cleanly instead of criss-crossing the map, and so the person best placed to do a run is the one who gets it.

The short answer: optimise visits in two moves. First, cluster them into geographic runs and sequence each run so a carer travels the shortest sensible path rather than a scatter of separate journeys. Second, match each run to the best-suited carer, scoring on distance, continuity with the client, spare hours and the right skills, not just who happens to be free. homecareOS does both automatically with run templates, travel-time intelligence and an AI rota builder, turning hours of manual planning into a schedule that is built in seconds and leaves real slack in the day.

What a run actually is

A run is simply a sequence of visits that belong together, usually because they are close in geography and time. Instead of treating a carer's day as a list of unrelated appointments, you treat it as one journey with several stops, planned as a whole. The difference sounds small and is not. A handful of visits placed in the wrong order can add an hour of driving to a shift; the same visits in the right order can hand that hour back.

Sequencing is where the time is won

Clustering visits by area is the easy half. The harder, more valuable half is the order. Two carers can be given the same six calls in the same patch and finish an hour apart, purely because one route doubles back and the other does not. This is a genuine optimisation problem, and it is exactly the kind of thing software does better than a person with a highlighter, because it can weigh every ordering at once against real travel times rather than a guess.

It matters that those travel times are honest. A plan built on optimistic, straight-line estimates falls apart the moment a carer meets a school run or a set of roadworks. Good route planning uses realistic journey times, so the run that looks tight on paper is tight in the world.

The best carer for the run, not just a free one

An efficient route assigned to the wrong carer is a false economy. If the carer does not know the client, or is not trained for the care the visit needs, you have saved travel time and lost something more important. So the second half of visit optimisation is matching: choosing the carer who fits the run best, not simply the first name with a gap in their diary.

homecareOS scores available carers on the things that actually matter: how close they are, whether they already know and are known by the client, how many hours they have left in a fair week, and whether they hold the right skills and training. Continuity in particular is not a nicety. For someone living with dementia, a familiar face is part of the care itself. The AI rota builder and cover finder surface the best fit in seconds, and the coordinator makes the final call.

The payoff: time given back to care

When runs are tight and well-matched, the wins stack up. Carers spend less of a paid shift driving and more of it delivering care, which is better for the people they visit and better for retention, because a day of endless driving is exactly the kind of grind that burns carers out. Fuel and mileage costs fall. And crucially, a well-planned day carries slack, the small cushion of time that lets an agency absorb a delay or a last-minute change without every visit behind it running late.

That slack is also what makes an agency resilient when something goes wrong on the day. A tight run is easier to protect than a fragile one, which is where real-time data and fast re-routing come in. We cover that side of the story in the companion piece on real-time events and decision intelligence.

Building runs in practice

  • Start from the care, not the staff. List the commissioned visits and their windows first, then build runs around them. A rota shaped by contracts rather than care needs hides its gaps until a client rings.
  • Use run templates. Most rounds repeat week to week. Save the good ones as templates so you build from a strong starting point rather than a blank grid every Monday.
  • Protect continuity as you optimise. The shortest route is not always the right one. Keep familiar carers with the same clients, and let the software optimise within that.
  • Leave honest travel time. Padding runs with realistic gaps is not inefficiency, it is what keeps the whole day on time.

Visit optimisation is quiet work. Done well, nobody notices it, visits simply happen on time, carers finish their runs without racing, and the miles that used to disappear into the day turn back into care. That is the whole point.
