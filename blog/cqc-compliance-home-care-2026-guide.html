CQC Compliance Made Simple: A Complete Guide for 2026

Navigating CQC requirements does not have to be stressful. This guide breaks down the Single Assessment Framework, Quality Statements, evidence categories, and the PIR — what's required, what's expected, and what your software should be doing for you.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 10 March 2026
This guide is for domiciliary (home care) providers. Residential care homes are also CQC-regulated but the evidence inspectors look at, and the operational realities behind it, are quite different. If you run a residential setting, Person Centred Software or Nourish are better suited.

Compliance is a daily output, not an annual project

The most common CQC mistake we see is treating compliance as a project — something you "do" before an inspection. It isn't. It's a continuous output of how your agency operates day to day. Inspectors don't care what binder you produced; they care what was happening on the ground at 7:15am last Tuesday.

That changes what good software does. The job of a home care platform under CQC is to capture the daily output — every visit, every note, every medication, every body-map mark, every safeguarding flag — in a way that produces inspection-ready evidence as a side-effect of normal work. If your software lets compliance evidence rot in spreadsheets and Word docs, the work falls back on managers in the four weeks before an inspection. That's the trap.

The Single Assessment Framework (SAF) in plain English

CQC moved to the SAF in 2023. The five Key Questions are unchanged from the previous framework — Safe, Effective, Caring, Responsive, Well-led — but how each is assessed changed. Each Key Question now contains a number of Quality Statements, and each Quality Statement has Evidence Categories attached.

The 34 Quality Statements (across all five Key Questions) describe, in short paragraphs, what good looks like. Examples:

  • Safe: "Safeguarding — We work with people to understand what being safe means to them as well as our partners on the best way to achieve this."
  • Effective: "Delivering evidence-based care and treatment — We plan and deliver people's care and treatment with them, including what is important and matters to them."
  • Caring: "Treating people as individuals — We treat people as individuals and make sure their care, support and treatment meets their needs and preferences."
  • Responsive: "Listening to and involving people — We make it easy for people to share feedback and ideas, or raise complaints about their care, treatment and support."
  • Well-led: "Capable, compassionate and inclusive leaders — We have inclusive leaders at all levels who understand the context the service operates in."

For each Quality Statement, the inspector is looking for evidence across six categories. The categories matter because they're what shapes the questions inspectors actually ask.

The six Evidence Categories

CategoryWhat it isWhere the evidence lives
People's ExperienceWhat service users and families actually say about the careFeedback forms, family portal, phone interviews, observed visits
Feedback from StaffWhat carers and managers say about working thereSupervisions, surveys, exit interviews, retention data
ObservationWhat an inspector sees during the visitOffice, home visits, calls observed live
Feedback from PartnersLocal authority commissioners, GP practices, district nursingCommissioner contact, complaint patterns, partnership minutes
ProcessesHow things are supposed to happen — your written systemsPolicies, procedures, risk assessments, audit cycle
OutcomesWhat actually happened — the measurable resultsVisit completion %, medication errors, falls, hospital admissions, complaints

The Process and Outcomes categories are where most home care software earns its keep. Processes = your platform documents what should happen. Outcomes = your platform records what did happen. Inspectors then compare the two.

How ratings work

Each Quality Statement gets one of four scores during inspection: 1 (Significant shortfalls), 2 (Shortfalls), 3 (No shortfalls), 4 (Exceptional). These roll up into a rating per Key Question, and ultimately an overall rating: Outstanding, Good, Requires Improvement, or Inadequate.

You don't need to score 4s to get Good. The bar is "no shortfalls" across most statements. Outstanding requires 4s in at least some statements, demonstrably exceptional practice, and consistently positive feedback.

The Provider Information Return (PIR)

Before an inspection (and sometimes between them), CQC asks for a PIR. It's a structured digital submission organised by Quality Statement. For each statement, you write a short narrative ("How we provide this") and back it with data ("evidence we'll share with you").

The PIR is where bad data hygiene catches up with you. If your visit data is in one system, training records in another, and complaints in a third, assembling the PIR is a four-week ordeal. If your visits, care notes, training, complaints, audits, and outcomes are in one platform, the PIR is a half-day exercise.

Areas where most agencies struggle on PIR:

  • Visit completion percentage — splits cleanly into planned / actual / missed / late buckets
  • Medication error rate per 1,000 administrations — needs MAR data and an incident log linked together
  • Training compliance percentage — by mandatory module, with expiry dates
  • Complaints resolved within 28 days — needs date-stamped complaints workflow
  • Service user feedback scores — collected systematically, not anecdotally

What inspectors actually look at on a domiciliary inspection

Based on patterns across recent inspections of domiciliary providers, inspectors typically follow this shape:

  1. Day before: Read the PIR, your last inspection report, recent notifications and complaints. Pick a sample of 6-12 service users.
  2. Office visit: Sit with the registered manager. Ask to see specific care plans, training records, complaint logs, audit cycle. Open the rota and pick three visits at random — ask to see what happened.
  3. Home visits: Visit 2-4 of the sampled service users at home. Watch a visit if consented. Ask the service user how they find the care.
  4. Phone interviews: Speak to 4-8 family members or representatives. Speak to 4-8 carers. Speak to a couple of partner organisations.
  5. Wrap-up: Feedback session with the registered manager. Score against Quality Statements. Draft report.

The most-asked questions in our experience: "Show me the care plan for [service user]." "What happened on this missed visit?" "Show me your last three medication errors and what changed afterwards." "How do you know carers are competent?" "When did you last review this person's care plan and why?"

For each of those, the platform should produce the evidence in seconds — not after a 30-minute search through files.

A note on workforce evidence

The Well-led key question often comes down to workforce stability. CQC inspectors look at sickness patterns, supervision frequency, training compliance, and turnover. Agencies tracking the Bradford Factor for absence patterns and using regular supervisions tend to score higher because they have evidence of noticing and acting on workforce signals. It's one of the most reliable separators between Good and Outstanding ratings on Well-led.

The four-nation note

This guide covers CQC (England). Wales (CIW), Scotland (Care Inspectorate), and Northern Ireland (RQIA) use different frameworks but the underlying principles overlap. Agencies that operate across nations should expect to maintain separate framework mappings; homecareOS handles all four with nation-specific templates that surface the right evidence per regulator.

If you're 4 weeks out from an inspection

Realistically, four weeks isn't enough to fix culture or rebuild systems. But it's enough to clean up evidence. Priority order:

  1. Pull the last 90 days of visits — confirm completion %, run an audit on missed visits and explanations
  2. Pull training compliance — get every expired or near-expiry module booked or extended
  3. Run a sample care plan audit — pick 10 service users, check care plan freshness, risk assessment review dates, body map currency
  4. Sample MAR records — pick 10 medication carers and check for gaps, untimed administrations, PRN protocols missing
  5. Spot-check complaint and incident logs — every entry has a date, a response, a closure
  6. Pre-fill PIR drafts — even rough drafts save days of panic

None of this is fixable with software in four weeks if it isn't already. But software that surfaces it in two clicks turns "we have a month of audit work" into "we have a week of audit work plus three weeks to actually improve the things that matter."

In summary

CQC compliance for domiciliary care comes down to: do the right thing every day, capture the evidence as you go, and have a platform that turns that evidence into PIR-ready answers. The framework is more transparent now than it was under KLOEs — Quality Statements are short and direct, Evidence Categories tell you what to surface. Software's job is to be the audit trail, not just the rota.

Frequently asked questions

What is the CQC Single Assessment Framework?

The Single Assessment Framework (SAF) is the inspection framework CQC has used since 2023, replacing the previous KLOEs approach. It assesses providers across the same five Key Questions (Safe, Effective, Caring, Responsive, Well-led) but breaks each into Quality Statements with associated Evidence Categories.

What are CQC Quality Statements?

Quality Statements are 34 short statements describing what good looks like (e.g. "We work effectively across teams and services to support people"). Each maps to one of the five Key Questions and has Evidence Categories attached: People's Experience, Feedback from Staff, Observation, Processes, and Outcomes.

How often does CQC inspect domiciliary care providers?

There is no fixed cycle. Inspections are risk-based — a 'Good' rating typically gets 2-3 years between full inspections, while 'Requires Improvement' or 'Inadequate' triggers faster re-inspection. CQC also runs targeted assessments without full inspection.

What is the Provider Information Return (PIR)?

The PIR is a structured submission CQC asks for ahead of inspection. It's organised by Quality Statement and asks for evidence backed by data. Agencies operating from a single platform tend to find PIR submission much faster than agencies with scattered systems.

Do home care agencies need DSCR-listed software?

DSCR (Digital Social Care Records) listing isn't legally required, but it's increasingly expected — local authority frameworks and ICB partnerships often require DSCR-listed providers. Most major UK home care platforms are now DSCR-listed; check the supplier's status before assuming.
