Supervision and appraisal for care workers (CQC Reg 18)

Regulation 18 says care workers must get the supervision and appraisal they need to do the job. It does not say how often. This guide sets out what the regulation and CQC's guidance actually require of a home care agency in England, how Skills for Care suggests you run sessions, and what an inspector will want to see in the file.

By Philip Martin · Founder, HomecareOS · Published 28 October 2026

Home care is lonely work. A carer can go weeks seeing the office only on the rota, spending each day in other people's homes and making judgement calls without a colleague in the room. Supervision is the planned time when someone sits down with them, looks at how the work is going and agrees what happens next.

It is also a regulatory requirement, and one of the first things an inspector looks for in a staff file. This guide covers regulated home care in England. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own regulators and standards.

What Regulation 18 says

Regulation 18 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 deals with staffing. Regulation 18(2)(a) says persons employed in the provision of a regulated activity must "receive such appropriate support, training, professional development, supervision and appraisal as is necessary to enable them to carry out the duties they are employed to perform".

CQC's guidance on Regulation 18 is the part providers must have regard to. On supervision and appraisal, it says:

  • providers must have an induction programme that prepares staff for their role
  • where appropriate, staff must be supervised until they can demonstrate the required level of competence to carry out their role unsupervised
  • staff should receive appropriate ongoing or periodic supervision in their role to make sure competence is maintained
  • staff should receive regular appraisal of their performance from an appropriately skilled and experienced person, and any training, learning and development needs should be identified, planned for and supported
  • staff must receive appropriate supervision to make sure they demonstrate and maintain competence in understanding the needs of people with a learning disability and autistic people
  • health, social and other care professionals must have access to clinical or professional supervision as their professional regulator requires.

CQC must refuse registration if a provider cannot satisfy it that it can and will comply with Regulation 18. CQC cannot prosecute for a breach of this regulation, but it can take other regulatory action.

How often supervision should happen

Neither the regulation nor CQC's guidance sets a frequency. The test is whether supervision is "appropriate" and enough to maintain competence, and whether appraisal is "regular".

That puts the decision on you. Write the frequency into your supervision policy, say why it suits your service, and set a separate, closer pattern for new starters who are still being supervised until competent. Then keep to it, because an inspector will compare what your policy promises with what your records show.

Skills for Care's effective supervision guide lists the warning signs of supervision that is not working. They include sessions that are often cancelled at short notice, sessions "infrequently scheduled" with "no fixed timeframe", and notes that are not written up or shared. Each of these is easy to spot from a staff file.

Where supervision shows up in a CQC assessment

Under CQC's single assessment framework, supervision is named in the safe and effective staffing quality statement, under the safe key question. CQC's explanation of that statement says staff receive the support they need to deliver safe care, including supervision, appraisal and support to develop, and that poor performance is managed appropriately.

Supervision is also a natural source of evidence for workforce wellbeing and enablement, under the caring key question. CQC says that statement means staff have regular opportunities to give feedback, raise concerns and suggest improvements, and are supported if they are struggling at work. A supervision session is one of the clearest places that happens, and a written note is how you show it did.

What a supervision session covers

Skills for Care describes three functions of supervision:

  • Supportive. Helping with challenges in the role, monitoring health and wellbeing, and dealing with anything that needs further investigation, such as performance concerns or safeguarding.
  • Line management. Making sure the carer understands their role, follows policies and procedures, and knows the standards expected of them.
  • Educational or developmental. Assessing skills and knowledge, identifying learning needs and agreeing how to meet them.

For a home care agency, a practical agenda that covers all three might be:

  • actions agreed at the last session, and whether they happened
  • how the carer's visits are going, including any clients they are worried about
  • anything from spot checks, care notes, missed or late visits, or feedback from clients and families
  • safeguarding concerns, incidents or near misses since the last session
  • training that is due, overdue or would help, and how the carer has used recent training
  • wellbeing, workload, travel and anything making the job harder
  • agreed actions, with who does what and by when.

Skills for Care publishes a free supervision recording template built around questions the supervisee answers, such as their biggest achievement since the last session, the challenges they have faced, the learning they have done and what they want to achieve next. It ends with feedback from the supervisor, agreed actions and two signatures.

Who can supervise

Supervision does not have to be done by the registered manager. Skills for Care's guide lists deputy managers, line managers and team leaders, senior or experienced care workers, internal and external trainers and registered nurses among the people who can supervise. It notes that good practice suggests staff should have at least one year's experience in their work before they are given a supervisory role in a care setting.

Whoever does it, the guide is clear that in regulated services the registered manager is responsible and accountable to CQC for making sure effective supervision takes place. Supervisors need supervision themselves. One of the warning signs Skills for Care lists is a supervisor who rarely gets their own.

Spot checks, induction and appraisal

These three sit alongside supervision and are often confused with it.

Spot checks are observations of a carer at work in someone's home, often unannounced. They are a different activity from a supervision session, but what you observe is good material for the next one. Skills for Care's guide quotes a CQC inspector describing a homecare agency rated outstanding in 2019: "Regular supervisions, annual appraisals and regular unannounced spot checks. We looked at evidence of supervisions and spot checks and found them to be comprehensive."

Induction is where supervision is most intensive. CQC's guidance says staff must be supervised until they can show the required competence to work unsupervised. In England most agencies structure induction around the Care Certificate, and Skills for Care notes that where an agency uses it, the registered manager has overall responsibility for signing off the standards.

Appraisal is the periodic look back at performance in the role and forward at development. CQC's guidance says it should be regular and carried out by an appropriately skilled and experienced person. Many agencies run it once a year, but that is a choice to set out in your own policy rather than a rule in the regulation.

What to keep on file

When an inspector samples a carer's file, the supervision trail should answer a few simple questions:

  • Does the carer have supervision and appraisal at the frequency your policy says?
  • Is there a written record of each session, signed or confirmed by both people?
  • Were the actions from one session followed up at the next?
  • Did concerns raised in supervision lead somewhere, such as training, a change to the rota, a safeguarding referral or performance management?
  • Did new starters get closer supervision until they were signed off as competent?

The records that answer those questions are the ones that link up: the supervision note, the training it led to, the spot check it discussed and the incident it followed. Kept in separate folders, they are hard to show together. Mandatory training gaps found in supervision should turn into booked training, and a safeguarding concern raised in a session should follow your safeguarding procedure.

Frequently asked questions

Is supervision a legal requirement for care workers?

Yes, in regulated home care in England. Regulation 18(2)(a) of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 says staff must receive such appropriate support, training, professional development, supervision and appraisal as is necessary to enable them to carry out the duties they are employed to perform.

How often should care workers have supervision?

Neither Regulation 18 nor CQC's guidance on it sets a frequency. CQC's guidance says staff should receive appropriate ongoing or periodic supervision to make sure competence is maintained, and regular appraisal. Set a frequency in your own supervision policy, explain why it suits your service, and then keep to it.

What is the difference between supervision and appraisal?

Supervision is a regular, planned conversation between a supervisor and a member of staff about their work, wellbeing and development. An appraisal is a periodic review of performance in the role, which CQC's guidance says should be carried out by an appropriately skilled and experienced person, with learning and development needs identified, planned for and supported.

Do spot checks count as supervision?

A spot check is an observation of a carer at work, and it is a different thing from a supervision session, although what you see in a spot check is useful to discuss in the next supervision. Skills for Care's guide quotes a CQC inspector describing an outstanding homecare agency that had regular supervisions, annual appraisals and regular unannounced spot checks, all evidenced.

Who can carry out supervision in a home care agency?

It does not have to be the registered manager. Skills for Care lists deputy managers, team leaders, senior and experienced care workers, trainers and registered nurses among the people who can supervise, and suggests at least one year's experience before someone takes on a supervisory role. The registered manager stays accountable to CQC for making sure effective supervision happens.
