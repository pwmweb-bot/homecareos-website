RQIA Compliance Software: Built for Northern Ireland Domiciliary Care

Northern Ireland has its own regulator, its own regulations, its own workforce register and its own criminal-record check. CQC and the Care Act stop at the Irish Sea. This guide sets out what RQIA, NISCC and AccessNI actually require of a domiciliary care agency in NI, and what a home care platform should be doing to carry that evidence for you.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 27 June 2026

If you run a domiciliary care agency in Belfast, Derry, Armagh or anywhere else in Northern Ireland, most of the "UK home care software" you'll be pitched was designed around England's CQC. That matters more than it sounds. The framework, the language and the evidence shape are all built for a regulator that has no jurisdiction over you. This piece is the Northern Ireland companion to our complete guide to home care software in the UK, written specifically for RQIA, NISCC and AccessNI, and honest about where England-only tools fall short.

First, the hard boundary: CQC and the Care Act are England-only

It's worth saying plainly because a surprising amount of marketing blurs it. The Care Quality Commission, the Single Assessment Framework, Quality Statements and the Care Act 2014 apply in England. None of them apply in Northern Ireland. If a vendor demos you a "CQC-ready dashboard" as your compliance home, they're showing you the wrong nation's paperwork. (For contrast, our separate CQC compliance guide covers how it works across the water, useful only if you also operate in England.)

In Northern Ireland the picture is its own: RQIA is the regulator, the Domiciliary Care Agencies Regulations (NI) 2007 plus the DHSSPS Minimum Standards set the bar, NISCC registers the workforce, and AccessNI does the vetting. Get software that speaks that language natively and compliance becomes a by-product of daily work rather than a translation exercise.

RQIA, the regulator, registration and the registered manager

The Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority (RQIA) is the independent body that registers and inspects health and social care services in Northern Ireland, including domiciliary care agencies. You cannot lawfully operate a domiciliary care agency in NI without being registered with RQIA.

Two roles sit at the heart of registration: the registered provider (the organisation or responsible individual) and the registered manager, a named, fit-and-proper person who is accountable for the day-to-day running of the agency. RQIA expects the registered manager to be visibly in charge: reviewing care, monitoring quality, managing incidents and driving improvement. When inspectors arrive, much of what they test is whether the registered manager actually knows what is happening on the ground, not just what the policy folder claims.

That's precisely where software earns its place. A platform that surfaces live visit status, overdue reviews, training gaps and open incidents gives the registered manager a real command position, the same operational grip you'd want before choosing any tool, which is why it sits near the top of our home care software buyer's checklist.

The legal framework: the 2007 Regulations and the DHSSPS Minimum Standards

Two documents define what "good" looks like in NI domiciliary care:

  • The Domiciliary Care Agencies Regulations (Northern Ireland) 2007, the statutory rules covering registration, the conduct of the agency, records, staffing, and the duty to review and improve quality.
  • The DHSSPS Domiciliary Care Agencies Minimum Standards, the standards RQIA inspects against, covering areas such as the assessment of need, the care plan, delivery of care, safeguarding, staffing and management. These are the NI equivalent in function to England's Quality Statements, but they are a separate document with their own structure and wording.

The practical upshot: your care records need to map to these standards. An assessment of need, a person-centred care plan that's reviewed and dated, daily records of the care actually delivered, medication records, and a clear audit trail behind every one. Software configured to the Minimum Standards captures that evidence as carers work, rather than leaving a manager to reverse-engineer it the week before an inspection.

NISCC, registering and monitoring your workforce

The Northern Ireland Social Care Council (NISCC) is the regulator for the social care workforce. Domiciliary care workers in NI must be registered with NISCC and must work to the NISCC Standards of Conduct and Practice for Social Care Workers. Registration is not a one-off tick: it must be kept in date and renewed, and registrants are expected to keep up post-registration training and learning.

For an agency, that creates an ongoing tracking job. Every carer has a NISCC registration (a PIN) with a renewal date. Let one lapse and you have a worker delivering regulated care who is not properly registered, exactly the kind of gap an inspection will find. So your software should treat NISCC registration as a first-class field: recorded per carer, alerting you before it expires, and reportable across the whole team in one view. Marry that to training-expiry alerts and you can evidence a workforce that is both registered and competent, which is what RQIA is really testing under the staffing and management standards.

AccessNI, the right check for the right nation

Criminal-record vetting in Northern Ireland runs through AccessNI, not the DBS. AccessNI issues three levels of disclosure:

  • Basic, unspent convictions only.
  • Standard, spent and unspent convictions, cautions and certain other disclosures.
  • Enhanced, the fullest check, for those in regulated activity, and able to include a check against the relevant barred lists.

Care workers delivering personal care will typically need an Enhanced AccessNI check. The point for software buyers is subtle but important: a DBS certificate number is not the correct record for an NI carer, and a platform that only has a "DBS" field is quietly recording the wrong evidence. You want dedicated AccessNI fields, level, certificate number, issue date, so your recruitment and staff files line up with what RQIA expects to see.

Inspections, notifications and the annual quality report

RQIA inspects domiciliary care agencies and, under the 2007 Regulations, the registered person must run a system to monitor and improve the quality of the service and produce reports on it, commonly handled as an annual quality report supported by ongoing quality monitoring. Separately, agencies must notify RQIA of certain reportable events: deaths, serious injuries, allegations of abuse and other significant incidents.

Both of these are data problems dressed up as paperwork. The quality report leans on visit completion figures, complaint handling, medication records, incident trends and service-user feedback. Notifications depend on incidents being logged promptly, with dates, actions and outcomes attached. If that information lives in three different systems and a WhatsApp group, both jobs become a scramble. If it lives in one platform, the quality report is largely an export and every notifiable event already has its record.

How homecareOS handles Northern Ireland

homecareOS treats the nation as a first-class attribute, not a cosmetic setting. When an agency onboards as Northern Ireland, the platform configures itself accordingly: NISCC as the workforce registration ID type, AccessNI as the vetting check, and NI care-record and compliance templates aligned to the Minimum Standards, never CQC Quality Statements or Care Act language. Training-expiry and registration-renewal alerts run against your actual carers, and the compliance hub assembles the evidence behind quality monitoring and notifications so your registered manager can pull it in seconds rather than days.

It's the same engine agencies use in the other three nations, CIW in Wales and the Care Inspectorate in Scotland, each with its own regulator, register and vetting scheme wired in. Built for home care, not care homes; and built for your regulator, not England's.

But the reason to choose it in NI isn't the price; it's that the compliance surface is the right one for where you actually operate.
