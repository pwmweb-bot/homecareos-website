The Future of UK Home Care: Five Forces and One Obligation

Home care in the United Kingdom is being remade by five forces arriving at once: a population ageing in its own homes, a funding settlement that has resisted fifteen years of reform, a workforce the sector cannot recruit fast enough, an analogue infrastructure being switched off beneath it, and a policy shift from reacting to crises toward preventing them. This is the pillar essay of our Future of Care series. It draws the threads of six detailed essays into one argument about where the sector is heading, and about the obligation, ethical as much as operational, that comes with getting there.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 25 August 2026

One sector, five converging forces

Predictions about social care have a poor track record, so this essay tries to do something more defensible: to describe the forces already in motion, whose direction is not seriously in dispute, and to reason about what they imply. Each force is examined in depth in its own essay in this series; what the pillar adds is the connections between them, because none of the five operates alone. Demography drives demand; demand collides with funding; funding constrains the workforce; workforce scarcity pushes the sector toward technology; and technology, used well, is what makes a preventative model of care possible at all.

Two numbers frame everything that follows. In England, healthy life expectancy at birth in 2021 to 2023 was roughly 61.5 years for males and 61.9 years for females, and it has fallen since 2017 to 2019 (ONS, 2024). Set against total life expectancy, that implies close to two decades of later life lived in less than good health, most of it at home. Meanwhile, Skills for Care projects that adult social care in England will need in the order of 470,000 additional posts by 2040 to keep pace with a growing over-65 population, against a backdrop of persistent vacancies and turnover (Skills for Care, 2024). Demand is rising on a curve; supply is not. Everything interesting about the future of home care follows from that gap.

The home becomes the primary setting for care

The first force is demographic and, just as importantly, preferential. For most of the twentieth century, serious frailty in later life led sooner or later to a hospital ward or a residential home. That default is inverting. Older people consistently say they want to remain in their own homes, and policy has followed them there: hospital-at-home services, virtual wards and discharge-to-assess models are deliberately moving clinical activity out of buildings and into front rooms. The result is that the home is becoming not an adjunct to the care system but its primary setting, which transforms domiciliary care from a peripheral service into critical infrastructure. It also creates a formidable operational problem that residential care never had: delivering safe, coordinated, accountable care across thousands of separate households, each with its own front door, its own family and its own risks. Our essay on ageing in place examines that shift in full: the demographic tide, the evidence on what older people actually want, and why coordination, not clinical capability, is the constraint that will decide whether care at home can scale.

The money question that never gets answered

The second force is fiscal, and it is the one on which the least progress has been made. For fifteen years, “fixing social care funding” has been the reform every government promises and none delivers: the Dilnot cap was legislated and postponed, the Health and Social Care Levy was introduced and repealed, and the underlying settlement, a means-tested system administered by councils under sustained budget pressure, remains broadly what it was. The consequences land directly on home care providers, who typically serve two customers at two prices: councils commissioning at rates that often sit below the full cost of care, and private clients cross-subsidising them. Yet the same essay finds genuine grounds for pragmatism: home care remains structurally cheaper than residential alternatives for many people, and there are practical levers, from commissioning reform to relief on the VAT providers pay on their overheads, that could ease the squeeze without waiting for the next white paper. Our companion piece on funding the future of social care works through the structural squeeze, the graveyard of reform attempts and the economics of home versus residential care in detail. Its conclusion matters for this pillar: the sector cannot plan on rescue arriving from Westminster, so efficiency and evidence of value are not optional extras; they are survival strategy.

A workforce the sector cannot recruit its way to

The third force is the most human. Adult social care in England employs around 1.59 million people and still carries a vacancy rate of 8.3%, roughly 131,000 unfilled posts, with turnover that corrodes the continuity older people value most (Skills for Care, 2024). The international recruitment that plugged the gap after Brexit has itself been narrowed by immigration policy, and the projected need for hundreds of thousands of additional posts by 2040 makes one conclusion hard to escape: the sector cannot simply recruit its way out of the demographic curve. That forces attention onto retention, and the evidence there is more hopeful than the headlines. Carers leave over pay, certainly, but also over unpaid travel time, chaotic rotas, feeling unsafe and feeling unseen; each of those is at least partly within an agency's control. Our essay on the future of the care workforce examines the scale of the shortage, the economics of pay and conditions, and what actually drives carers to stay. Its central claim is one this pillar endorses: technology's honest role is augmentation, removing the friction and unpaid administration that burn carers out, and it is not a substitute for fair reward.

The digital deadline under everything else

The fourth force is infrastructural, and it comes with a date attached. The UK's copper telephone network is being withdrawn, and with it the analogue signalling that an estimated 1.7 million telecare users quietly depend on; the risks were serious enough that the migration timetable was pushed back toward 2027, and Ofcom now requires telecoms providers to identify and protect telecare users through the transition (Ofcom, 2023; Hamblin and Whitfield, 2025). At the same time, the record itself has gone digital at remarkable speed: around 80% of adult social care providers now use a digital social care record, covering more than 90% of people receiving care, up from under 40% in 2022 (NHS England, 2025a). The next frontier is interoperability, connecting those records to the NHS systems around them so that a carer's observation can inform a GP's decision. Our essay on technology-enabled care and the analogue switch-off treats this as one story rather than three: the phone line, the telecare alarm and the paper folder are all being replaced at once, and providers who manage that transition as an operational programme, rather than letting it happen to them, will carry their service users across safely. The risks it refuses to wave away, digital exclusion, rural connectivity and cyber-security, are real, and they recur below as ethical questions, not just technical ones.

From reacting to crises to preventing them

The fifth force is a change in what care is for. NHS England's population health management agenda describes a system that segments populations by risk, spots deterioration early and intervenes before the ambulance is called (NHS England, 2025b). Home care is uniquely placed to serve that ambition, because carers observe frail people repeatedly, in their own environment, precisely where deterioration begins. But the evidence counsels humility as well as hope. The largest UK trial of home telehealth found lower mortality and fewer emergency admissions, but disappointing cost-effectiveness (Steventon et al., 2012); risk-stratification tools introduced without a plan for acting on them have made things worse rather than better; and the most robust preventative signal in the literature is strikingly low-tech: a systematic review found that greater continuity of care was associated with significantly lower mortality in 18 of 22 higher-quality studies (Pereira Gray et al., 2018). Continuity is something a home care agency directly controls through how it allocates visits. Our essay on predictive and preventative care weighs the whole evidence base and lands on a deliberately unglamorous conclusion: the biggest preventative gains will come from doing ordinary things consistently, capturing observations well, keeping familiar faces in front of the same people, and noticing change early.

The obligation: ethics as a condition, not a footnote

Running through all five forces is a sixth thread that behaves less like a trend and more like a test. Digital tools are moving into the most intimate spaces of people's lives: bedrooms, bathrooms, medication rounds. A sensor that keeps someone safer can also watch them more closely than they wish; consent given at the anxious moment of a hospital discharge is not the same as freely chosen, ongoing agreement; and the people most dependent on care are disproportionately on the wrong side of the digital divide, a divide a House of Lords committee found to be persistent and patterned by age, disability, income and region (House of Lords, 2023). The Nuffield Council on Bioethics argues that autonomy, dignity and consent in care technology are not settled by demonstrating clinical benefit or meeting the legal minimum; they require active, continuing judgement (Nuffield Council on Bioethics, 2023). Our essay on the ethics of care technology works through surveillance and dignity, the Mental Capacity Act, the special sensitivity of care data and the risk of algorithmic dehumanisation. Its standard is the one this whole series holds itself to: technology must serve the human relationship at the centre of care, and where it erodes that relationship it has failed, whatever its metrics say.

Where software fits, honestly stated

If the five forces above are broadly right, the role of software in home care's future is significant but bounded. Software will not fix the funding settlement, and it will not pay carers more. What it can do is attack the gap between demand and supply from the inside: recovering the hours agencies lose to paper, phone-tag scheduling and duplicated records; protecting continuity of care when visits are reallocated; keeping structured observations so that change is noticed early; and carrying providers over the digital deadline with records that are already interoperable by design. Those are the problems homecareOS was built for, as an operational platform for UK agencies across all four regulatory nations (CQC in England, CIW in Wales, the Care Inspectorate in Scotland and RQIA in Northern Ireland), and the research shaping those choices, from continuity evidence to workforce studies, is published openly on our evidence page. But the claim this essay is prepared to defend is deliberately modest: good software is a necessary condition for the future described here, not a sufficient one. The sector's future will be decided by people, money and policy; software's job is to stop administration consuming the time and attention that care itself needs.

What the next decade asks of providers

Pull the threads together and the shape of the next decade is reasonably clear. More care will happen at home, delivered to older, frailer people with more complex needs. Public funding will remain tight, so providers will need to evidence value and run lean. The workforce will stay scarce, which makes retention, and everything that drives it, the central management discipline of the sector. The infrastructure beneath care will finish going digital, deadline and all. And the services that thrive will be those that use the resulting data to prevent crises rather than merely record them, while treating the people they serve as citizens with rights rather than data points with risk scores.

None of this is speculative; every element is already under way. What remains open is how well the transition is managed, and that is largely in the hands of the thousands of agencies that actually deliver care. The six essays in this series are an attempt to give that work a serious evidence base: ageing in place, funding, the workforce, the digital transition, prevention and ethics. Read together, they make one argument: the future of UK home care is not something that will happen to the sector. It is something the sector will build, visit by visit, in other people's homes.
