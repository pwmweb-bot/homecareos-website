How to Start a Home Care Agency in the UK: A Practical 10-Step Guide

A regulator-aware, founder-honest guide to setting up a UK domiciliary care agency in 2026. Covers the four-nation registration process (CQC, CIW, Care Inspectorate, RQIA), realistic costs (£8-25k), what banks and the regulator actually want to see in your business plan, the first hires that genuinely matter, and the software you need from day one. Written for first-time founders, not franchise pitches.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 9 June 2026

The reality check before you start

UK home care is a £10 billion-a-year market, growing roughly 5% annually, with chronic carer shortages and a demographic tailwind that doesn't quit. There's never been a better time to enter, and never been a tougher time to run one. Margins are slim (5-15% net is normal), regulator scrutiny has tightened post-pandemic, and the recruitment market for carers is bare-cupboard difficult.

The agencies that succeed in 2026 share three things: they pick a regulator-correct setup from the start, they hire a Registered Manager who's done it before, and they invest in operational software early so admin time doesn't strangle the founder. Almost every agency that fails fails on cash flow, not demand.

This guide is the practical 10-step version. There's no franchise pitch at the end. If you want to pay £35,000 to a franchise and follow their playbook, that's a valid choice, but most successful UK home care founders go independent because the margins matter and the playbook isn't worth £35k. What follows is what an independent founder actually needs to do.

Step 1: Pick your nation and regulator

Everything that follows depends on which UK regulator you'll register with. The four:

  • England: Care Quality Commission (CQC). No fee to apply, but every registered provider pays an annual fee once registered, based on how many people you support. CQC publishes no turnaround standard, so plan for several months. About 85% of UK home care providers sit under CQC. Full walkthrough: CQC registration for a home care agency.
  • Wales: Care Inspectorate Wales (CIW). Operates under RISCA 2016. No CIW registration fee and no annual fee; your workers register with Social Care Wales instead. Applications typically take up to 14 weeks. See CIW registration in Wales.
  • Scotland: Care Inspectorate. The only nation with an upfront fee to register: a care at home application starts at £1,261 for a small service, banded by staff numbers, with an annual continuation fee on top. Background checks are PVG via Disclosure Scotland, and staff register with the SSSC, not DBS. See Care Inspectorate registration in Scotland.
  • Northern Ireland: RQIA. No RQIA registration fee and no annual fee. AccessNI for background checks; NISCC for staff registration. See RQIA registration in Northern Ireland.

The regulator you pick is not "which one looks easiest". It's the one your registered address sits under. If you live in Cardiff you register with CIW. If you live in Glasgow you register with the Care Inspectorate. Pick the one that matches your geography. Every figure above changes over time, so confirm the current position on the regulator's own site before you budget.

For a deeper look at how the four regulators shape what software and processes you need, see our complete guide to home care software in the UK. The four-nation section is the bit most other guides skip.

Step 2: Choose your service model

"Home care agency" isn't a single thing. The four common service models, with very different regulatory and commercial shapes:

  • Visiting domiciliary care. Short visits (15-min to 1 hour) for personal care, medication, meal prep, social calls. The classic model. Highest visit volume, tightest margins per minute.
  • Live-in care. A carer lives at the service user's home for blocks of typically 1-4 weeks. Premium pricing (£900-£1,500/week typical), fewer service users, very different operational shape.
  • Supported living. Care in tenants' rented housing (usually for adults with learning disabilities or autism). Separate landlord-tenancy arrangement. Different fee structure (often LA-block-funded) and different regulator inspection focus.
  • Companionship-only / non-regulated. If you're not delivering personal care, you may not need to register. But the moment one client asks for medication prompts or personal hygiene support, you're regulated. Build for the regulated case from day one.

The most common starting model is visiting domiciliary care, private-pay-heavy in the first year, with council-commissioned work added later once you have a track record. We'll assume that shape for the rest of this guide.

Step 3: Write the business plan

You need a business plan for three audiences: the regulator (parts of it become your Statement of Purpose), your bank (if you want a business account or working-capital facility), and yourself (because the act of writing it surfaces the things you haven't thought about).

What it needs to contain at minimum:

  • Statement of Purpose. Required by every UK regulator. Who you are, who you serve, what services you offer, your aims and objectives. ~10 pages, becomes a living document the regulator references at every inspection.
  • Market analysis. Catchment area, demographics, competitor density, fee benchmarks for private and council work in your area.
  • Service offer and pricing. Hourly rates by visit type, weekend/bank holiday premiums, live-in rates if applicable. Be realistic: typical UK domiciliary rates 2026 are £22-£35/hour private, £18-£25/hour council.
  • 3-year financial projections. P&L, cash flow, and especially a sensible carer cost model (national living wage + 25-30% on-costs for employer NI, pension and holiday pay).
  • Recruitment plan. The bottleneck for almost every new agency. How will you find your first 5-10 carers? Sponsorship licence (Health and Care Worker visa) if relevant?
  • Marketing plan. Referral channels, digital presence, partnerships with local authority discharge teams, GP surgeries, hospitals.
  • Risk and contingency plan. What if a key carer leaves? What if a service user safeguarding concern hits? What's your reserve fund?

Banks specifically want to see realistic cash flow showing you understand local-authority payment terms (typically 30-60 days from invoice).

Step 4: Register the business entity

Most home care agencies operate as limited companies. The reasons: liability protection, tax efficiency once you're profitable, credibility with commissioners, and easier ownership transitions later.

  • Companies House. Register the limited company. £50 online, takes 24 hours.
  • HMRC. Register for Corporation Tax. Register as an employer for PAYE (you'll have employees from day one).
  • ICO. Register with the Information Commissioner's Office for data protection. £40-£60/year. Mandatory because you'll handle health-related personal data.
  • Business bank account. Most providers (Tide, Starling, Barclays Business) approve within 1-2 weeks. Open this before you start applying for the regulator licence. They'll ask for proof of an active business account.
  • Accountant. Engage one. Care has specific VAT exemption rules and pension auto-enrolment thresholds you don't want to miss.

Step 5: Apply for regulator registration

This is the longest step. Three things get registered at once: the legal entity (your limited company), the accountable person (the Nominated Individual in England, the Responsible Individual in Wales, with Scotland and Northern Ireland using their own arrangements), and the Registered Manager (the person responsible for day-to-day quality and safety). How long it takes varies a lot by nation: CQC publishes no service standard and runs to several months, CIW aims for up to 14 weeks, the Care Inspectorate aims for six months, and RQIA is commonly quoted at 8 to 12 weeks for a well-prepared application.

The process, documents, fees and traps differ enough by nation that each has its own deep dive: England (CQC), Wales (CIW), Scotland (Care Inspectorate) and Northern Ireland (RQIA). The essentials below apply everywhere.

What you'll need to submit

  • Statement of Purpose (from your business plan)
  • Evidence of your Registered Manager's qualifications (a Level 5 Diploma in Leadership and Management for Adult Care or equivalent) and prior care management experience
  • Enhanced background checks for the accountable person and Registered Manager (DBS in England and Wales, PVG in Scotland, AccessNI in Northern Ireland)
  • References, fitness-to-practice declarations, health declarations
  • Evidence of insurance arrangements
  • Premises information (even if you're operating from home)
  • Policies and procedures (a library of around 40 documents)

The Registered Manager interview

CQC and the Care Inspectorate will interview your Registered Manager (CIW and RQIA do similar Fit Person interviews). The interview is the single most common point of delay or rejection. The interviewer is testing whether your RM understands the regulations, the service offer, and how they'd handle realistic operational scenarios, not whether they've memorised the regulations word for word. Practical experience always reads better than textbook recital.

Common rejection reasons

  • Statement of Purpose mismatch with reality (e.g. claims to cover 24/7 but no on-call plan)
  • Registered Manager underqualified or inexperienced for the proposed service shape
  • Insufficient policy library, particularly safeguarding, medication and infection control
  • Unclear separation of Nominated Individual and Registered Manager responsibilities

If your application is rejected you can amend and resubmit, but be warned: in England a resubmission counts as a brand new application and loses its place in the queue, so a rejection effectively restarts the clock. That is why getting the Statement of Purpose and the Registered Manager right first time matters so much.

Step 6: Insurance, policies, and compliance setup

Three mandatory insurance lines:

  • Public Liability: £5-10 million cover. £200-£500/year for a small agency.
  • Employer's Liability: £10 million minimum (legally required if you have employees). £200-£500/year.
  • Professional Indemnity: £1-5 million. £300-£800/year.

Specialist brokers (PolicyBee, Towergate, Hiscox) understand the home care sector and price more sensibly than generalist insurers.

Then the policy library. Every regulator expects you to have ~40 written policies covering safeguarding, medication management, infection control, equality and diversity, complaints, whistleblowing, lone working, health and safety, business continuity, GDPR/data protection, plus role-specific procedures. Options:

  • Buy a policy pack. £500-£1,500 one-off, available from QCS, Care Hub, Hempsons. Fastest path. Customise to match your Statement of Purpose.
  • Write your own. Free but realistically 40-60 hours of founder time. Only worth it if you genuinely know what good looks like.
  • Use software-included policies. Some home care platforms (including homecareOS) include a maintained policy library as part of the subscription, with auto-updates as regulations change.

Whichever route, the policies need to live somewhere your team can actually access them, not in a binder gathering dust in your office.

Step 7: First hires: Registered Manager, Coordinator, Carers

The three roles, in priority order:

The Registered Manager (mandatory before you can deliver care)

Unless you're a one-person owner-operator with the Level 5 yourself, your Registered Manager is the most important hire of the first year. Realistic salary £35,000-£50,000 depending on location and experience. They sign off compliance, they take the regulator interview, they own the inspection outcome.

If your budget is tight, options include hiring part-time (3 days/week) initially or sharing an RM with a sister business. Both work; both have downsides the regulator will ask you to explain at inspection.

The Care Coordinator

The operational engine. Builds the rota, allocates carers, deals with sickness, takes service-user calls. £25,000-£32,000. A good coordinator is the difference between a calm agency and a chaotic one. You can do this role yourself for the first 5-10 service users; past that you need a dedicated person.

Your first carers

Five to ten carers is enough to start. Realistic salary £11.50-£14/hour (above national living wage, mostly to attract retention). Every carer needs:

  • DBS enhanced check (PVG in Scotland, AccessNI in NI)
  • SSSC / SCW / NISCC registration depending on nation
  • Right-to-work check
  • Two satisfactory references
  • Care Certificate induction (15 standards) before delivering unsupervised care
  • Mandatory training: safeguarding, infection control, moving and handling, medication, basic life support, fire safety, GDPR

If you're recruiting from outside the UK on the Health and Care Worker visa, you'll need a sponsorship licence (£574 fee 2026) and a certificate of sponsorship per worker. This pathway has tightened significantly in 2024-2026; budget time and legal advice.

Step 8: Software and operations from day one

You can run a 2-carer agency on a paper diary, WhatsApp, and a spreadsheet. You cannot run a 5-carer agency that way, and you certainly cannot pass a CQC inspection that way. Set up your operational software before you take your first visit.

The non-negotiables a new agency needs from day one:

  • Drag-and-drop rota with carer-service-user assignment
  • Mobile carer app with GPS-verified check-in/out and care notes (offline-capable, because UK signal isn't guaranteed)
  • Electronic care plans and daily notes
  • eMAR (electronic medication administration records)
  • Training and DBS expiry tracking
  • Invoice generation at a minimum; payroll export ideally
  • Compliance dashboard aligned to your regulator's evidence shape

For a new agency under five carers, the realistic choice is between a free starter tier (so software cost is £0 until you grow) or a small monthly subscription (£40-£100/month for a 5-carer paid plan on most platforms).

Step 9: Win your first clients

There are three routes into your first dozen service users. Most agencies use all three at different intensities.

Private-pay (direct families)

Highest margin (£25-£35/hour typical), most flexibility, but slow to build because trust takes referrals. Channels: Google Local pack ranking, Care Quality Commission directory listing, GP surgery noticeboards, local Facebook community groups, the Homecare Association consumer-facing directory.

Local-authority commissioned

Lower margin (£18-£25/hour), higher volume, predictable. But you typically need 3-6 months of operating history before councils will award block contracts. Get on the council's "approved provider" framework as early as possible. Most allow new providers to apply for the framework before they have visits delivered.

NHS-funded

Continuing Healthcare (CHC) and Section 117 placements. Often higher rates than local-authority work but more clinically complex service users. Build relationships with hospital discharge teams and Integrated Care Boards (ICBs).

The strongest single referral source most agencies overlook: discharge planners at local hospitals. They're under constant pressure to discharge medically-fit patients home. A reliable home care agency that picks up the phone at 4pm on a Friday is a discharge planner's favourite agency. Build that relationship from week one.

Step 10: Survive year one

The three things that kill new agencies in year one:

  1. Cash flow. Councils pay 30-60 days after invoice. Private clients pay weekly or fortnightly. Carers and HMRC don't wait. Budget at least 3 months of fully-loaded payroll as working capital, typically £5,000-£15,000 depending on payroll size. The single biggest line in your startup budget.
  2. Carer retention. National turnover is 28-35% annually (Skills for Care 2024 State of Care). Every leaver costs £3,000-£5,000 in replacement, training and lost productivity. Track Bradford Factor from day one to spot patterns. Pay above national living wage. Pay travel time. Pay mileage at HMRC rates (45p first 10,000 miles, 25p thereafter).
  3. Regulator complacency. Don't wait until your first inspection to organise the evidence. Keep your CQC (or CIW/CI/RQIA) evidence current from day one. Inspectors look for living documents; they spot retrofit a mile off.

The second coordinator hire usually comes at 20-25 active carers. Don't delay it. By the time you "need" a second coordinator, you're already burnt out and missing things.

The bottom line

Starting a UK home care agency in 2026 is genuinely viable for first-time founders who get the regulatory setup right, hire a Registered Manager with relevant experience, and budget honestly for the cash-flow runway. The market demand is real, the margins are workable, and the operational complexity is manageable once you put the right systems in from day one.

The biggest mistakes new founders make are: underestimating CQC/CIW/CI/RQIA timelines (budget 12-16 weeks from application to live), undercapitalising the working-capital cushion (budget 3-6 months payroll), and skimping on operational software (it costs more in admin hours than the subscription would have cost).

Take the 10 steps in order. Don't try to start delivering care before your regulator registration is approved (you're committing a criminal offence if you do). And whatever software you choose, set it up before your first visit, not after.
