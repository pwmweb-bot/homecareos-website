Technology-Enabled Care and the Analogue Switch-Off: Social Care’s Digital Deadline

Three transitions are arriving in UK home care at once, and they share a single logic: the analogue infrastructure that quietly underpinned care for decades is being switched off. The public telephone network is going digital, the telecare alarms plugged into it must follow, and the paper records that once lived in a folder on the kitchen table are giving way to digital social care records expected to connect to the NHS. This essay examines that convergence, the technology-enabled care agenda and its looming digital deadline, and asks, soberly, what it demands of providers and of the people they care for.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 17 July 2026

A deadline hiding in the phone line

Technology-enabled care (TEC) is the umbrella term for the sensors, alarms, apps and connected devices that help people live more safely and independently at home. It is not new: pendant alarms and pull-cords have been a fixture of community care for forty years. But the ground beneath it is shifting. The reassurance offered by a careline alarm rested on an invisible assumption: that a working analogue telephone line sat behind it. That assumption is being retired.

This is part of a wider re-platforming of care from analogue to digital that also encompasses the record itself. The home care sector is large, stretched and fragmented: adult social care in England alone employs around 1.59 million people, carries an 8.3% vacancy rate of roughly 131,000 posts, and contributes an estimated £68.1 billion in gross value added (Skills for Care 2024). A change of infrastructure on this scale is not a background upgrade; it is an operational event that the sector must actively manage. This piece is one of several in our Future of UK home care series, and it takes the infrastructure angle deliberately: not what a digital care plan should contain, but what has to be true underneath it for care to keep working.

The analogue switch-off and the telecare it strands

The United Kingdom's copper-based Public Switched Telephone Network (PSTN) is being withdrawn, with voice services migrating to digital connections carried over broadband. For most households this is a modest change. For the estimated hundreds of thousands of people who rely on telecare (a fall detector, a pendant alarm, an epilepsy or wandering sensor), it is anything but, because a great many of those devices were engineered to signal down an analogue line and do not behave predictably on a digital one.

Ofcom has set expectations for the telecommunications companies managing the migration. Providers are required to identify and protect telecare users, to avoid migrating them unless their device is known to be compatible with a digital line, and to provide at least one hour of power resilience so that an emergency call can still be made during a power cut (Ofcom 2023). The last of these matters more than it first appears: an analogue line drew power from the exchange and kept working when the lights went out, whereas a broadband router sitting on a shelf does not, so a resident who has fallen in a blackout could find both their lighting and their lifeline gone at once.

Academic analysis of the switchover is sobering about how this dependency went unnoticed for so long. Hamblin and Whitfield (2025) describe telecare's "hidden" reliance on analogue signalling and, more troublingly, a diffusion of responsibility for protecting vulnerable users spread thinly across telecoms firms, local authorities, care providers and families, with no single actor clearly accountable. It is partly this fragmentation of responsibility that led to the migration timetable being paused and pushed back to 2027, buying time but not resolving the underlying problem. For a home care agency, the practical implication is direct: the provider is often the party that actually knows which service users depend on a careline, and is therefore drawn into a coordination role it was never formally assigned.

From reactive alarms to ambient sensing

The switch-off is also an inflection point in what TEC is for. The pendant alarm is a reactive device: it waits for a person to press a button after something has already gone wrong. The newer generation of connected devices (movement sensors, smart-home monitors, wearables) promises something more anticipatory: detecting the gradual changes that precede a crisis, so that a visit or a conversation can happen before a fall or an admission does. This is the same shift toward anticipation explored in our companion essay on predictive and preventative care.

The evidence for these technologies is real but should be read with care. The largest UK randomised trial of home telehealth, the Whole System Demonstrator, studied more than 3,000 patients across 179 general practices and associated the intervention with lower mortality and fewer emergency admissions, yet found little robust evidence of cost savings, and its headline mortality finding was subsequently contested (Steventon et al. 2012). The honest reading is that connected care can help, but the size and durability of the benefit depend heavily on how it is implemented and who it is offered to; it is not a guaranteed dividend that follows automatically from installing a device.

There is an ethical dimension that clinical results alone cannot settle. Monitoring technologies that keep an older person safer can also watch them more closely than they might wish, and consent given at the anxious point of a hospital discharge is not the same as freely chosen, ongoing agreement. The Nuffield Council on Bioethics (2023) argues that autonomy, consent, surveillance and dignity are not resolved by demonstrating clinical benefit or by meeting the minimum of legal compliance. They require active, continuing judgement. A sensor that reduces risk while eroding a person's sense of privacy in their own home has not straightforwardly improved their care.

The record goes digital: from paper to DSCR

The second strand of the transition concerns the record itself. The national Digital Social Care Records (DSCR) programme has driven a rapid move away from paper. Around 80% of the roughly 18,000 adult social care providers now use a digital social care record, covering more than 90% of people receiving care, up from under 40% in 2022 (NHS England 2025). Whatever one thinks of the pace, this is one of the faster digitisations of a frontline service the country has seen, and it means paper is no longer the default against which digital is judged; increasingly it is the exception that must be justified.

The independent evidence is encouraging but appropriately qualified. A rapid evaluation for the National Institute for Health and Care Research found that digital records can save administrative time and improve access to information, but that these benefits are contingent: they depend on training, connectivity and ongoing support, and the costs of adoption and the risks to equity weigh most heavily on smaller providers (Malley et al. 2026). This is the recurring theme of the whole transition: the technology rarely fails on its own terms; it fails where the surrounding conditions (a reliable connection, a trained workforce, a supplier that answers the phone) are absent. Digital care planning and electronic medication records are the clearest early wins, and we examine each in depth in our pieces on the future of digital care plans and eMAR software for home care.

Why the systems have to talk to each other

Digitising a record is necessary but not sufficient. A care note trapped inside one provider's system, invisible to the GP, the district nurse and the hospital, digitises the silo rather than dissolving it. The value of a digital record is unlocked only when it can be shared safely, with consent, and in a form the receiving system can read.

This is the purpose of shared care records, which bring together information from primary care, community services, mental health, social care and secondary care into a single view for the professionals involved in a person's care. NHS England (2023) is developing standards-based routes to connect social care into that wider picture, including a Social Care Interoperability Platform intended to let social care records exchange information with the health system rather than sit outside it. Interoperability, in this sense, is not a feature to be bolted on later; it is the difference between a digital record that merely replaces a paper one and a digital record that changes what coordinated care can do. For a home care provider, the strategic question is no longer only "have we gone digital?" but "can our system speak the standards the NHS is building toward?"

The risks the sector must not wave away

An honest account has to hold the promise alongside three serious risks.

Digital exclusion. The people most likely to depend on care are also disproportionately likely to be on the wrong side of the digital divide. A House of Lords committee found digital exclusion to be persistent and patterned by age, disability, income and region, and warned that as services migrate online the excluded are pushed further to the margins rather than lifted along with everyone else (House of Lords 2023). A transition that improves care for the connected while stranding the disconnected would deepen the very inequalities social care exists to address.

Rural connectivity. The digital replacement for an analogue line assumes a decent broadband connection, and that assumption does not hold everywhere. Full-fibre broadband reaches 52% of rural premises against 71% of urban ones, and around 48,000 premises still lack a decent fixed broadband connection at all (Ofcom 2024). For agencies covering dispersed rural rounds, this is not an abstraction: it is the difference between a device that signals reliably and one that fails silently, and between a carer's app that syncs and one that cannot.

Cyber-security. Digitising care concentrates sensitive personal data in systems that become worth attacking. A landscape study commissioned by the Department of Health and Social Care found adult social care providers, often small, with limited in-house IT expertise, materially exposed to cyber risk while holding exactly the kind of sensitive data that makes a breach so damaging (DHSC 2025). The move to digital does not merely add a security task to the list; it makes security a duty of care in its own right.

How homecareOS is built for a digital-first future

This is the environment homecareOS was designed for, and it is worth being explicit about where the platform sits in the transition described above, as a practical embodiment of it, not a cure for it.

Digital-first and paper-free by construction. homecareOS keeps care plans, daily notes, body maps and electronic medication administration records as structured digital data from the outset, with an immutable audit trail: care records are never edited or deleted, which is both a regulatory requirement and the foundation of a trustworthy history. Our digital care records are built to be the primary record, not a scan of a paper one.

Offline-capable, so a weak signal is not a failure. The rural connectivity gap is a design constraint, not an edge case. The homecareOS carer mobile app is offline-first: a carer can capture notes and sign a medication record on a visit with no signal, and the device syncs when connectivity returns, with no loss of data. In a sector where nearly one in two rural premises still lacks full fibre, a record system that only works online is a record system that will sometimes not work at all.

Built toward interoperability and four-nation compliance. Because the visit and the record are structured data rather than free-form documents, the platform is positioned to connect to the standards-based shared-care routes the NHS is building, rather than remaining a silo. And because nation is a first-class attribute of every agency, the same system reflects the CQC framework in England, CIW in Wales, the Care Inspectorate in Scotland and RQIA in Northern Ireland, so a provider is not force-fitting one country's rules onto another's workflow as the digital agenda advances across all four nations at once.

None of this removes the hard parts of the transition: the telecare audit, the staff training, the connectivity contract, the security discipline. It does mean the record layer, at least, is already on the right side of the switch-off. If you are choosing that layer now, our guide to software for domiciliary care in the UK sets out what to look for.

The bottom line

The analogue switch-off, the spread of technology-enabled care, and the move from paper to interoperable digital records are not three separate stories but one: the infrastructure of home care is being rebuilt in digital form, and the deadline is real even where it has been delayed. The opportunity is a sector that can anticipate rather than merely react, and coordinate rather than duplicate. The obligation is to carry the excluded, the rural and the vulnerable across with everyone else, and to treat security as care rather than as compliance. Providers that treat this as an operational programme to be managed, not a background upgrade that will happen to them, will be the ones still standing, and still trusted, when the copper is finally gone.
