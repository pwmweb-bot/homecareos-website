Home Care vs Care Home Software: What's Actually Different, and Which Do You Need?

"Home care" and "care home" are two words apart and a whole operating model apart. One is built on visits, travel and records captured in other people's houses; the other on shifts, floors and life inside one building. This guide sets out the real operational differences between the two settings, what those differences mean when you choose software, and an honest account of where homecareOS fits and where it doesn't.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 25 August 2026
The short answer: Home care software and care home software solve different problems, and the difference starts with the unit of work. Domiciliary (home care) platforms are built around the visit: hundreds of short calls a day, carers travelling between addresses, and records captured on a phone in someone's own home, often offline. Residential (care home) platforms are built around the shift: staff rostered to a building, occupancy and rooms, and round-the-clock records for people who live on site. The two settings even register differently with regulators; in England a domiciliary agency registers with CQC for the regulated activity of "personal care", while a care home registers for "accommodation for persons who require nursing or personal care". homecareOS is built domiciliary-first, with rota, care plans, eMAR and compliance evidence all designed around the visit. If you run a residential or nursing home, a residential-specific system will usually serve you better; if you deliver care in people's own homes, visit-based software is the right foundation.

Two services that share a vocabulary, and almost nothing else

People search for "care home software" when they mean home care, and "home care software" when they mean a care home, constantly. The confusion is understandable: both sectors talk about care plans, rotas, MAR charts, safeguarding and inspections. But underneath the shared vocabulary sit two different businesses. A domiciliary agency sells time in other people's homes, delivered by a workforce that spends its day on the road. A residential home sells accommodation and round-the-clock care in a building it controls. Nearly every operational decision, and therefore nearly every software decision, flows from that split. If you're at the start of a buying process, our complete guide to home care software covers the domiciliary side in depth; this article is about the boundary between the two.

Visits vs shifts: the scheduling divide

The deepest difference is the unit of scheduled work. In domiciliary care it's the visit: a 30-minute morning call, a 45-minute lunch, a double-up for a hoist transfer. A mid-sized agency might deliver several hundred visits a day, each with its own time window, tasks, and continuity expectations, and each preceded by a journey. The rota is really a routing problem wearing a scheduling problem's clothes: travel time between addresses is as much a part of the plan as the care itself, and one late visit cascades down a carer's whole run. That's why domiciliary scheduling software is built around run templates, travel-time awareness and GPS check-in and check-out at the door.

In a care home the unit is the shift. Staff are rostered to the building, or to a floor or unit within it, in blocks of eight or twelve hours. Nobody travels between service users; the residents are all under one roof. The hard problems are different: safe staffing ratios by dependency, skill mix across the shift (who holds the medication competency, who is the senior on nights), agency cover, and handover between shifts. A care home rota that treated every interaction with a resident as a scheduled visit would collapse under its own weight; a domiciliary rota that only knew about shifts couldn't tell you whether Mrs Evans's 8am call was missed.

Travel vs floors

Travel isn't just a scheduling input for home care, it's a cost line, a pay question and a compliance question. Mileage has to be claimed and paid, travel time has to be accounted for in pay to stay on the right side of minimum wage rules, and the geography of a round shapes who can realistically cover a call when someone phones in sick. Software for domiciliary care has to treat the map as a first-class citizen.

A care home's equivalent concerns are spatial in a different way: which floor a resident lives on, which rooms need two staff, where the nurse call activity is concentrated tonight. That drives features like unit-based rostering, call-bell integration and room-level records that make no sense in the community, and that visit-based platforms sensibly don't have.

MAR in the community vs in-house medication rounds

Medication is where the settings diverge most sharply in practice. In domiciliary care, the medicines live in the service user's own home, dispensed by their own community pharmacy, sometimes managed jointly with family. The carer arrives, prompts or administers what is due at that visit, records the outcome, and leaves. The record has to be captured on a phone, work offline in signal blackspots, and cope with the reality that a different carer may attend the next call. We've written a full guide to eMAR for home care covering what that demands of software.

A care home runs medication as an in-house operation: stock ordered and received centrally, stored in a locked treatment room or trolley, administered on rounds by staff with medication competency, with nurse-led clinical administration in nursing homes. Stock control, ordering cycles, fridge temperature logs and trolley audits are core workflows. It is genuinely a different discipline, closer to a small pharmacy operation than to a carer supporting someone with their own blister pack at their kitchen table, and medication software built for one setting transplants poorly into the other.

Different regulated activities, different evidence

The regulatory split is not cosmetic. In England, a domiciliary agency registers with CQC for the regulated activity of personal care, while a care home registers for accommodation for persons who require nursing or personal care. That second registration pulls the building itself into scope: premises and equipment, nutrition and hydration, the environment residents live in. A home care inspection asks how you evidence safe care delivered behind dozens of front doors you don't control; a care home inspection walks the corridors of a building you do.

The same line is drawn in every nation: CIW in Wales registers domiciliary support services and care home services separately under RISCA, the Care Inspectorate in Scotland distinguishes care at home from care home services, and RQIA in Northern Ireland inspects domiciliary care agencies and residential and nursing homes against separate standards. Compliance software has to mirror the framework you're actually inspected under. Our CQC compliance guide covers the domiciliary side of that in detail, and the homecareOS compliance module is built specifically around the domiciliary frameworks in all four UK nations.

What this means when you choose software

The practical rule is simple: match the software's data model to your service model. Ask any vendor what the basic unit of their system is. If the answer is the visit, it's domiciliary software. If the answer is the shift, the room or the resident-day, it's residential software. Everything else, the care plan editor, the reporting, the compliance dashboards, is built on top of that foundation, and no amount of configuration changes the foundation.

Buying across the line costs you in predictable ways. A domiciliary agency on shift-based software ends up tracking visits in spreadsheets beside the system, loses missed-call alerting, and pays carers from timesheets rather than actual door-to-door times. A care home on visit-based software drowns in artificial "visits" that don't reflect how care actually happens on a floor, and gets no help with occupancy, dependency or stock. Both end up with software that has to be worked around rather than worked with.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use care home software for a domiciliary care agency?

Technically yes, practically no. Care home software models shifts, rooms and occupancy; a domiciliary agency needs to model visits, travel between addresses, and records captured on a phone in someone's home, often with no signal. The same mismatch applies in reverse. The rota, the medication records and the inspection evidence all flow from the system's basic unit of work, so the operational model of the software should match the operational model of the service.

Is homecareOS suitable for residential care homes?

No, and we say so plainly. homecareOS is built domiciliary-first, from the visit-based rota to eMAR to compliance evidence, all designed around carers travelling to people in their own homes. A residential or nursing home needs shift-based rostering, occupancy management, in-house medication stock control and records built around one building, and a residential-specific system will serve that far better. If you deliver care in people's own homes, homecareOS is built for exactly that.

Do regulators treat home care and care homes differently?

Yes, in every UK nation. In England, domiciliary agencies register with CQC for "personal care" while care homes register for "accommodation for persons who require nursing or personal care", which brings premises, nutrition and environment into scope. Wales draws the same line under CIW, Scotland separates care at home from care home services under the Care Inspectorate, and RQIA in Northern Ireland regulates the two settings under separate standards. The evidence inspectors ask for differs accordingly.

What if my organisation runs both home care and a care home?

Mixed providers usually end up running two systems, one per setting, and that is often the right answer rather than a procurement failure. Each service is registered, inspected and staffed differently, so forcing both into one platform tends to compromise both. Choose the strongest system for each setting and keep shared functions like payroll and HR joined up at the back office. Some suppliers claim to cover both settings in one product; test the weaker half of that claim hard before you believe it.
