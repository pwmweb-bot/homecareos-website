Pre-Employment Health Questionnaire for Care Workers

CQC expects you to be satisfied that every carer is fit for the work. The Equality Act says you generally cannot ask about their health until you have offered them the job. This guide shows a home care agency how to do both, in the right order, without putting off good applicants.

By Philip Martin · Founder, HomecareOS · Published 26 November 2026

Every home care agency in England has to be satisfied that the carers it sends into people's homes are fit to do the work. Regulation 19 says so, and CQC will look for evidence on the staff file. But the Equality Act 2010 sets a firm rule about when you can ask about health: in most cases, not until you have offered the job.

Get the order wrong and you risk a discrimination claim, action by the Equality and Human Rights Commission, and losing applicants who could have been excellent carers. Get it right and the health questionnaire becomes what it should be: a short, fair step after the offer that helps you put the right support in place before the first visit.

This guide is for registered managers and recruitment coordinators. It covers England for the CQC parts; Section 60 of the Equality Act applies in England, Scotland and Wales. It sits beside our care worker recruitment checklist and safer recruitment in domiciliary care guides. It is not legal advice, and it does not replace your own recruitment policy or advice from an HR adviser or occupational health service.

What CQC expects on health

Regulation 19 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 is the fit and proper persons employed duty. Regulation 19(1)(c) says people employed must:

be able by reason of their health, after reasonable adjustments are made, of properly performing tasks which are intrinsic to the work for which they are employed.

Schedule 3, which lists the information you must hold for each person employed, includes (at paragraph 8) satisfactory information about any physical or mental health conditions relevant to the person's capability, after reasonable adjustments, to properly perform tasks intrinsic to their role.

CQC's guidance on Regulation 19 adds three points worth reading slowly:

  • All reasonable steps must be taken to make adjustments so people can carry out their role, in line with the Equality Act 2010. This may include offering alternative roles.
  • This part of the regulation does not mean that people who have a long-term condition or a disability cannot be appointed.
  • Selection and interview processes should meet the requirements of the Equality Act 2010 in relation to pre-employment health checks.

So CQC is not asking for a medical history. It is asking whether you have considered, fairly, whether this person can do the intrinsic tasks of this role, with adjustments where they need them.

The timing rule: Section 60 of the Equality Act

Section 60 of the Equality Act 2010 says that an employer must not ask about an applicant's health before offering them work, or before putting them into a pool of people it intends to offer work to. Whether someone has a disability counts as part of their health for this purpose.

Two details matter for home care agencies:

  • The offer can be conditional. Section 60 treats a conditional offer as an offer. The EHRC's guidance for employers on pre-employment health questions says you can make an offer conditional on medical checks and then ask health-related questions without breaching Section 60.
  • It covers questions to other people, not just the applicant. The same guidance says the rule applies to written and spoken questions put to third parties, such as a current or former employer.

The EHRC explains the purpose plainly: to stop health or disability information being used to sift people out before they have had the chance to show they have the skills to do the job.

The exceptions that matter in home care

Section 60(6) allows some health-related questions before an offer, but only so far as they are necessary for one of these purposes:

  • Adjustments to your recruitment process. You can ask whether an applicant needs adjustments to take part in an interview, assessment or test, such as extra time, an accessible room or a different format. The EHRC says this information should be collected separately from the rest of the application.
  • Intrinsic functions of the job. You can ask whether the applicant will be able to carry out a function that is intrinsic to the work. Where you reasonably believe a duty to make reasonable adjustments would apply, Section 60(7) says you read "intrinsic" as the function once those adjustments are made. The EHRC's example is a construction company asking scaffolders whether they have a condition that affects their ability to climb ladders.
  • Diversity monitoring. You can ask monitoring questions, but the EHRC says these should be kept separate from the application and not seen by the people making shortlisting or interview decisions.
  • Positive action, such as a guaranteed interview scheme for disabled applicants, and the rare case where having a particular disability is an occupational requirement.

The intrinsic-function exception is narrow. It does not open the door to a general health form on your application. The EHRC gives the example of a standard application form asking about health issues and sickness days in the last two years, and says that breaches Section 60.

If you do rely on an exception, keep the question tied to the task. "Home care visits involve supporting people to move, using equipment such as hoists after training. Is there anything that would stop you doing this, with or without adjustments?" is about the job. "Do you have any back problems?" is about the person's medical history.

References and sickness absence

This is where many agencies trip up without realising. A reference request that asks "How many days' sickness did the applicant have?" or "Do you know of any health problems?" is a health question put to a third party.

The EHRC checklist says that if you seek references before an offer, do not ask for information about sickness absence. Its guidance gives the example of a recruitment agency shortlisting candidates partly on sickness figures from referees, and says that breaches Section 60.

Schedule 3 still expects satisfactory evidence of conduct in previous care work and verification of why care roles ended. You can ask about conduct, reliability and reasons for leaving without asking about health. Our care worker references guide covers what to ask instead.

Interviews

The same principle applies in the interview room. The EHRC's guidance shows the line clearly:

  • Asking an applicant to describe how they handled a difficult situation is about their ability to do the job, not their health, and is fine.
  • Asking whether they have health issues that would affect their ability to cope with the pressures of the job, where coping with that pressure is not an intrinsic function, is a breach.
  • If an applicant volunteers information about a condition, you can ask follow-up questions about reasonable adjustments for intrinsic tasks. The EHRC suggests explaining why you are asking.

Build your interview around the work: dignity, consent, lone working, time management, recording, safeguarding. Our care worker interview questions guide has examples that stay on the right side of this.

What a care worker health questionnaire should cover

Once you have made a conditional offer, you can ask your health questions. Section 60 no longer stops you asking, but the Equality Act still protects the person from discrimination because of disability, and data protection law still expects you to collect only what you need.

A good questionnaire for a home care role is built from the job description, not from a list of illnesses. Start by listing the tasks that are genuinely intrinsic to the role you are offering, for example:

  • supporting people with personal care and moving and handling, after training
  • working alone in people's homes
  • driving between visits, where the role needs it (see our driving checks for care workers guide)
  • early, late, night or weekend shifts, where the role includes them (see care worker shift patterns)
  • work that brings contact with infection risks and the need to follow infection prevention and control procedures

Then ask, for each one, whether there is anything that affects the person's ability to do it and whether any adjustments or support would help. Leave space for the person to tell you what works for them. Questions framed this way give the registered manager what Schedule 3 asks for, without collecting detail you have no use for.

Keep the form separate from the information your interview panel used, and tell the applicant why you are asking, who will see the answers and how they will be used.

Reasonable adjustments, Access to Work and occupational health

The duty to make reasonable adjustments runs through the whole process, from the job advert to the first year in post. GOV.UK's guide to reasonable adjustments for workers with disabilities or health conditions gives examples such as changing the recruitment process so a candidate can be considered, doing things another way, changing equipment and allowing flexible or part-time hours. Acas's disability at work advice covers the employer's side in more detail.

In home care, adjustments might mean different visit types, a rota that avoids certain shifts, a walking or cycling round instead of a driving one, or extra time on certain tasks. Whether an adjustment is reasonable depends on your circumstances, so record what you considered and why.

The government's Access to Work scheme can help a disabled person get or stay in work, for example with a grant towards specialist equipment, a support worker, travel costs or mental health support. GOV.UK is clear that it does not pay for the reasonable adjustments an employer must legally make, but it can sit alongside them.

When answers raise a genuine question about an intrinsic task, refer the person to an occupational health adviser rather than making the call yourself. The EHRC recommends referring candidates to occupational health only after a job offer, and giving the practitioner instructions that discourage adverse assumptions about disability and support proper consideration of adjustments.

Be very careful about withdrawing an offer. The EHRC's guidance says a candidate could bring a disability discrimination claim if an offer is withdrawn because of their disability. If you reach that point, take advice first.

Handling health information safely

Health information is special category data. The ICO's page on what special category data is explains that "data concerning health" falls within it and needs particular care. The ICO also publishes employer guidance on information about workers' health under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018.

In practice:

  • collect only what you need for the role
  • limit access to the people who need it, usually the registered manager and whoever handles adjustments
  • keep the questionnaire with the staff file, not in a shared inbox or a coordinator's downloads folder
  • explain to the carer how the information will be used and how long you will keep it, in line with your privacy notice and retention policy

Fitness is not a one-off check

Regulation 19(5) and CQC's guidance expect you to regularly review the fitness of employees and respond fairly and promptly when concerns arise. Health can change. A carer who was fit for every task at appointment may need adjustments after an injury, an illness or a change in circumstances.

Supervision, return to work meetings and wellbeing conversations are where most of this happens. Our guides on supervision and appraisal, care worker sickness absence and care worker wellbeing cover those conversations.

A simple process for a home care agency

  1. Job advert and application: no health questions. Offer to make adjustments to the application or interview, and ask anyone who needs one to tell you.
  2. Before interview: if you need to, ask separately about adjustments for the interview or any assessment.
  3. Interview and references: focus on skills, values, conduct and reasons for leaving. No sickness absence questions to referees before an offer.
  4. Conditional offer: make it subject to satisfactory checks, including the health questionnaire, DBS, references and right to work.
  5. Health questionnaire: role-based questions, reviewed by someone competent. Refer to occupational health where needed.
  6. Adjustments: agree and record them before the first visit, including any rota or task changes.
  7. Before unsupervised work: confirm the Schedule 3 information is complete, as CQC's guidance says it must be confirmed before the person is employed. Then plan induction and shadowing, as in our onboarding new carers guide.
  8. Ongoing: review fitness through supervision and return to work meetings, with fresh adjustments where needed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I ask a care worker about their health before offering them the job?

Generally no. Section 60 of the Equality Act 2010 says an employer must not ask about an applicant's health before offering work, unless a narrow exception applies, such as finding out whether they need adjustments for an interview or assessment, or whether they can carry out a function that is intrinsic to the job. The offer can be conditional, so you can make it subject to satisfactory health checks and ask your questions then.

Does CQC require a health questionnaire for care workers?

The regulations do not name a form. Regulation 19 says people employed must be able, by reason of their health and after reasonable adjustments, to properly perform tasks intrinsic to their work, and Schedule 3 asks for satisfactory information about any physical or mental health conditions relevant to that. A health questionnaire completed after a conditional offer is the usual way agencies gather and record that information.

Can I ask a referee how many sick days the applicant had?

Not before you make an offer. The Equality and Human Rights Commission's Section 60 guidance says the rule covers questions put to third parties, including current or former employers, and its checklist says that if you seek references before an offer, you should not ask about sickness absence.

Can I withdraw a job offer because of the health questionnaire?

Be very careful. The EHRC's guidance says a candidate could claim disability discrimination if an offer is withdrawn because of their disability. Consider reasonable adjustments first, take occupational health advice where answers raise a real question about intrinsic tasks, and record your reasoning. CQC's guidance says having a long-term condition or a disability does not mean someone cannot be appointed.

Is health information on a carer's file special category data?

Yes. The ICO explains that data concerning health is special category data under UK GDPR, which needs extra care. Collect only what you need for the role, limit who can see it, and keep it secure with the rest of the staff file.
