Care Worker Probation Period: A Guide for Home Care

A probation period is where a new carer either settles in or slips away. This guide shows a home care agency how to set one up, run fair reviews, extend or end it properly, and prepare for the change to unfair dismissal rules in January 2027.

By Philip Martin · Founder, HomecareOS · Published 16 November 2026

Recruiting a carer takes weeks of adverts, interviews and checks. The probation period is where that work pays off, or where it is lost. A new starter who feels unsupported in their first months often leaves quietly. One who is struggling but never told so may pass by default and become a problem later.

This guide is for registered managers and owners of UK home care agencies. It covers what probation is for, how to set it up, how to run reviews, when to extend it, how to end it fairly, and what the Employment Rights Act 2025 changes from January 2027. It is not legal advice. For anything contentious, take HR or legal advice before you act.

What a probation period is for

Acas describes probation as a time at the start of employment to check whether someone is suitable for a job. It works both ways: the employer assesses performance, skills and fit, and the employee checks whether the role and organisation are right for them.

Two points from Acas are worth knowing from the start:

  • There is no legal requirement to have a probation period, and it can be any length.
  • Day one rights still apply. Protection from discrimination and from detriment for whistleblowing, and the right to join a trade union, apply during probation. Carers can take sick leave and are entitled to sick pay during probation too.

In home care, probation has an extra job. It is part of how you show you have checked a new carer can do the work safely before they visit people alone.

Why it matters to CQC

Regulation 19: Fit and proper persons employed says staff must have the qualifications, competence, skills and experience needed for their work. CQC's guidance says providers should assess competence before people work unsupervised, provide appropriate direct or indirect supervision until they are assessed as competent, and expects providers employing social care support workers to follow the Care Certificate standards.

Regulation 19 also expects a procedure for ongoing monitoring of staff, and arrangements to deal with staff who are no longer fit for their duties. Where someone no longer meets the requirements, the provider must take action that is necessary and proportionate.

Regulation 18: Staffing adds that staff must get the support, training, supervision and appraisal they need. A well-run probation, with recorded reviews and supervision, gives you evidence for both.

For what has to be in place before a carer starts, see our guide to safer recruitment in domiciliary care and the care worker recruitment checklist. Probation picks up where those checks end.

Setting up probation: write it down first

Acas's guidance on following a fair process says employers must set out the terms and conditions of probation in the written statement of employment particulars or the contract. If the detail sits in a policy, the statement or contract must say so.

For a care worker, that means being clear about:

  • Length. Three or six months is common, but choose what suits the role. Allow time to complete induction and the Care Certificate standards and to see the carer across different visits, people and times of day.
  • What "passing" looks like. Acas suggests clear expectations, such as meeting objectives. For a carer this might be signed-off competence in moving and handling and medication support, reliable attendance, accurate care notes, following the care plan, and positive feedback from the people they support.
  • Review points. When the reviews happen and who holds them.
  • Extension. Whether probation can be extended, and on what conditions.
  • Notice. The notice period that applies during probation.

Acas also recommends training managers in handling probation, so they can hold difficult conversations, resolve problems early and explain possible outcomes clearly. In a small agency that might be one registered manager and a care coordinator. Both should know the process.

Treat carers consistently, but be flexible where it is fair. Acas notes that part-time working, travelling for work, sickness, bereavement and reasonable adjustments for disabled employees can all affect how a probation should run. Home care has plenty of all of these.

Reviews: the heart of a fair probation

Acas describes probation reviews as formal checkpoints to check progress, discuss wellbeing, give feedback and let the employee raise concerns. Reviews should be one-to-one meetings, with regular informal check-ins in between, so the outcome is never a surprise.

Skills for Care's induction toolkit gives a useful rhythm for social care:

  • Week one and month one. An early supervision meeting to explain probation and review points, then regular supervision. The month one guidance suggests agreeing objectives and a development plan.
  • Around three months. The months three to six guidance recommends a formal review to assess progress against the month-one objectives, discuss strengths, identify concerns and agree support for the next stage.
  • The end of probation. A final review to confirm the new starter has met their induction objectives, shown the knowledge, skills and behaviours the role needs, and reached the competence to continue in post.

Before each review

Acas suggests the manager reviews the expectations, gathers feedback and performance information, and shares it with the employee beforehand so they can prepare. In home care, useful evidence includes:

  • spot checks and observed practice, including medication support
  • the quality of care notes and records
  • punctuality and missed or short visits, from your call monitoring
  • feedback from the people they support, families and colleagues
  • training completed and Care Certificate progress
  • supervision notes since the last review

During and after

Keep it a two-way conversation. Ask how they are finding lone working, travel and the rota, not just how they are performing. Be honest about concerns and specific about what needs to change. Acas advises keeping records of every review and sharing them with the employee afterwards, in line with UK GDPR.

If a carer is doing well, say so. A clear "you are on track" in month three is one of the cheapest retention tools you have. Our guide to employee engagement in home care covers more ways to make new starters feel valued.

Extending probation

Acas says an employer might extend probation when performance is not yet satisfactory but is likely to improve with more support or training. It advises:

  • having clear conditions for extension in your probation policy
  • confirming the extension before the original probation ends, as failing to do so could be a breach of contract
  • telling the employee in writing, which is required if probation terms are in their contract
  • updating the written statement within one month
  • agreeing the length of the extension, review points, expectations including any training, when the final review will be, and the possible outcomes

Absence needs care. Acas notes that an employer can use reasonable evidence of performance from before a period of sickness to pass someone, and must not discriminate in how extensions due to absence are handled. For disabled carers, reasonable adjustments apply.

Skills for Care's guidance makes the same point for social care: if more time or support is needed, discuss whether an extension is appropriate and agree a clear development plan.

When probation does not work out

Acas says dismissal during probation should be a last resort, after steps such as performance management or an extension. If you do dismiss, give the reasons in writing and the correct notice, either statutory or contractual. Employees have a right to request to be accompanied to meetings that could result in dismissal.

Skills for Care advises that where there are significant concerns despite support, you follow your HR policies and take HR advice before making employment decisions.

Safeguarding concerns are different

Failing probation for timekeeping or poor note-writing is a performance issue. Concerns about harm are not. The DBS guidance on making barring referrals says regulated activity providers have a legal duty to refer when two conditions are met. The first is that you remove the person from regulated activity, or would have done had they not resigned or left. The second involves relevant conduct, the harm test, or a caution or conviction for a relevant offence. The guidance says failing to refer without reasonable justification is an offence. Follow your safeguarding procedure, and read the DBS guidance in full for any such case.

The Employment Rights Act 2025 and probation

According to Acas, the Employment Rights Act 2025 does not affect probation periods. There is still no legal requirement to have one, and they can be any length.

What changes is unfair dismissal. From 1 January 2027, protection from unfair dismissal becomes a right after 6 months in a job. Someone who already has at least 6 months' service on 1 January 2027 will be protected.

Acas notes that employers may want probation to end before someone gains that protection, but warns this does not avoid legal risk, because other protections apply during probation. It also says probations of six months or less may not suit every role.

For home care agencies, the practical message is simple. Hold your reviews on time, record them, and make your decision before the probation end date. A process that is fair and well documented protects the people you support, the carer and the agency, whatever the qualifying period.

A simple probation checklist

WhenWhat to do
Before day oneProbation length, expectations, review points and extension terms in the written statement or contract
Week oneEarly supervision: explain probation, review dates and who to ask for help
Month oneAgree objectives and a development plan; record supervision
OngoingShadowing, spot checks, Care Certificate progress, informal check-ins
Around month threeFormal review against objectives; agree support; share the record
Before the end dateFinal review: confirm, extend in writing, or follow your process to end employment
AfterwardsWelcome them properly, set the next supervision, and log what you learned

Frequently asked questions

Is a probation period a legal requirement for care workers?

No. Acas says there is no legal requirement to have a probation period and it can be any length. Most home care agencies use one because it gives a structured way to check a new carer's competence, reliability and fit, and to support them while they settle in.

How long should a care worker probation period be?

There is no set length. Many agencies choose three or six months. Pick a length that gives enough time to complete induction, assess competence against the Care Certificate standards and see the carer across a range of visits, and write it in the contract or written statement.

Does the Employment Rights Act 2025 change probation periods?

Acas says the Act does not affect probation periods. What changes is unfair dismissal. From 1 January 2027, protection from unfair dismissal becomes a right after 6 months in a job. Acas warns that ending probation before that point does not remove legal risk, so a fair process still matters.

Can you extend a care worker's probation?

Yes, if your contract or policy allows it. Acas advises confirming the extension in writing before the original probation ends, agreeing the length, review points and expectations, and updating the written statement within one month.

Do we have to refer a carer to the DBS if they fail probation?

Not just for failing probation. The legal duty to refer applies when you remove someone from regulated activity, or would have done had they not left, and the conduct or harm conditions in the DBS guidance are met. Poor timekeeping alone would not normally meet them. Check the DBS guidance for any case involving harm or risk of harm.
