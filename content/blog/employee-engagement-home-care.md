Ask most home care managers what keeps them up at night and staffing comes near the top. Recruitment gets a lot of attention, but every carer you keep is one you do not have to replace. Employee engagement is how you keep them.

This guide explains what engagement means in a domiciliary care setting, why it is harder than in a care home, and the practical things a small or medium agency can do about it: staff surveys, supervision, fair rotas, communication and recognition.

## What employee engagement means in home care

Engagement is how connected, motivated and committed people feel to their work. For a carer, that is three things: the people they support, the colleagues they work with, and the agency that employs them.

Home care makes the third one hard. Carers often start and finish at home, travel between visits alone and might only see the office for training or supervision. Their whole picture of your agency comes from the rota, the messages they get and the few conversations they have with the office. If those are rushed, unclear or only happen when something is wrong, engagement drops, however much they love the people they visit.

Skills for Care's guidance on [staff wellbeing, rewards and recognition](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Staff-wellbeing-rewards-and-recognition.aspx) puts it plainly: adult social care roles can be physically and emotionally demanding, and without the right support staff may experience stress, burnout and reduced engagement.

## Why it matters to CQC, not just to your rota

Engagement is not a soft extra. Several parts of the regulatory framework touch on it.

- [Regulation 18: Staffing](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-18) says staff must receive the support, training, professional development, supervision and appraisal they need to carry out their role.
- The [Safe and effective staffing](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/assessment/single-assessment-framework/safe/safe-effective-staffing) quality statement expects staff to receive the support they need to deliver safe care, including supervision, appraisal and support to develop.
- The [Freedom to speak up](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/assessment/single-assessment-framework/well-led/freedom-speak-up) quality statement looks for a positive culture where people feel they can speak up and that their voice will be heard.
- The [Shared direction and culture](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/assessment/single-assessment-framework/well-led/shared-direction-culture) quality statement expects staff to know and support the service's vision and values, and leaders to show a positive, compassionate, listening culture.

An inspector who talks to your carers will quickly get a sense of whether they feel listened to. Engagement work gives you the evidence to back that up.

## 1. Ask, with a short staff survey

You cannot fix what you do not know about. A short, anonymous survey run two or three times a year gives every carer a voice, including the quiet ones who never ring the office.

Keep it short enough to finish on a phone between visits. Ten questions is plenty. A simple 1 to 5 scale works well, with one or two free-text boxes. Useful areas to cover:

- **Support:** "I know who to call if something goes wrong on a visit, and they answer."
- **Rota:** "My rota is fair and I get enough notice of changes."
- **Time:** "I usually have enough time on visits and between them."
- **Recognition:** "When I do good work, someone notices."
- **Development:** "I have had a useful supervision in the last three months."
- **Voice:** "I feel able to raise a concern without it being held against me."
- **Recommendation:** "I would recommend this agency to a friend as a place to work."

Then do the part most agencies skip. Share the results with the team, pick two or three things to change, and tell carers what you did. A "you said, we did" message after each survey shows carers it was worth answering. Run the same questions each time so you can see whether things are getting better.

## 2. Make supervision a conversation, not a form

Supervision is the one guaranteed private conversation a carer has with someone senior. Done well, it is your strongest engagement tool. Done as a tick-box exercise, it tells carers the opposite.

Skills for Care suggests normalising wellbeing and recognition conversations by building them into supervision and team meetings. In practice:

- Start by asking how the carer is, and listen before moving to the agenda.
- Ask what is getting in the way of good visits: travel time, a hard call, missing equipment, unclear care plans.
- Pass on specific positive feedback from families, people you support or colleagues.
- Agree one or two development goals, such as a qualification, a lead role or shadowing a senior carer.
- Follow up on what was raised last time, so carers see that supervision leads somewhere.

Our guide to [supervision and appraisal for care workers](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html) covers frequency, structure and recording in more detail.

## 3. Make the rota fair and predictable

For a home care carer, the rota is the agency. Skills for Care's guidance says that when shifts are inconsistent or income is uncertain, staff are more likely to feel dissatisfied and consider leaving, and it recommends offering consistent rotas and working hours where possible and applying rotas, workloads, pay and overtime consistently.

Practical steps:

- **Keep runs stable.** The same carers visiting the same people builds relationships on both sides.
- **Give notice.** Publish rotas as far ahead as you can, and keep last-minute changes to genuine emergencies.
- **Share the hard calls.** Rotate weekends, late finishes and difficult runs fairly, and be open about how you decide.
- **Plan realistic travel.** Squeezed travel time is stressful and, if it is unpaid, can also be a pay issue. Our guide to [National Minimum Wage and travel time](/blog/national-minimum-wage-travel-time-home-care.html) explains the rules.
- **Ask about preferences.** Caring responsibilities, school runs and second jobs all matter. Skills for Care recommends involving staff in decisions about working arrangements.

## 4. Communicate like a team, even when apart

Carers who only hear from the office when something has gone wrong stop picking up the phone. Aim for regular, two-way contact that is not just about problems.

- A short weekly update: new starters, people who have left the service, policy changes, and a thank-you or two.
- Team meetings at a time carers can actually attend, with a summary for those who cannot.
- A named person for every carer to call, and an on-call number that is answered out of hours.
- Quick replies to questions about pay, holidays and rotas. Slow answers on pay damage trust faster than almost anything else.
- A clear way to raise concerns, including anonymously, in line with CQC's expectations on [speaking up](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/assessment/single-assessment-framework/well-led/freedom-speak-up).

## 5. Recognise people properly

Recognition works best when it is specific, timely and fair. "Thanks for all your hard work" is nice. "Mrs Patel's daughter rang to say how calm you were when her mum fell on Tuesday, thank you" is remembered.

Low-cost ideas that suit home care:

- **Pass compliments on the same day,** and note them in the carer's record for supervision.
- **Mark milestones:** end of probation, completing the Care Certificate, a qualification, or years of service.
- **Give experienced carers a role,** such as mentoring new starters, leading on a topic like medication or dementia, or helping with interviews.
- **Celebrate as a team,** for example a monthly shout-out in your update chosen with input from colleagues.
- **Be fair.** Recognise night and weekend carers and people on rural runs, not only those the office sees most.

Skills for Care recommends using a blend of informal and structured approaches, including incentives, milestones and shared feedback, to reinforce value, purpose and pride. It also stresses that while pay is important, everyday actions that show staff they are valued can make a real difference.

## 6. Support wellbeing early

Home care can be lonely and emotionally heavy, especially after a death, a safeguarding concern or a difficult visit. Skills for Care's guidance suggests offering accessible support such as counselling or an employee assistance programme, responding promptly to concerns, training managers to spot stress, and offering flexibility during personal challenges.

A simple habit helps: after any difficult event, someone from the office calls the carer the same day to check in. It costs ten minutes and tells them they are not on their own. Skills for Care's [wellbeing resources](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Support-for-leaders-and-managers/Managing-people/Wellbeing/Wellbeing.aspx) also point to local wellbeing offers you can share with staff.

## 7. Start engagement before day one

Engagement starts with recruitment. A slow, confusing application process, or a new starter who does not hear from you for a fortnight while checks run, sets the tone before the first shift. ## How to tell whether it is working

Engagement is hard to see directly, so track a few signals you already have, month by month:

- Carer turnover, especially leavers in the first six months. Our guide to [calculating carer turnover](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html) shows how.
- Sickness absence and short-notice dropped shifts.
- How many supervisions happened on time.
- Survey scores on the same questions over time.
- How many new applicants were referred by your own carers.

None of these alone tells the whole story, but together they show whether things are moving the right way. Skills for Care's [Building a workforce that stays](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Building-a-workforce-that-stays.aspx) guidance is a useful place to look for more ideas once you know where the gaps are. ## Common mistakes

- **Surveying and then going quiet.** Asking for views and doing nothing is worse than not asking.
- **Recognition that only reaches the office favourites.** Carers notice who gets thanked.
- **Cancelling supervision when it gets busy.** It sends the message that carers come last.
- **Treating engagement as a one-off event.** A summer barbecue does not make up for an unfair rota all year.
- **Only calling carers when something is wrong.** Make sure good news travels too.
