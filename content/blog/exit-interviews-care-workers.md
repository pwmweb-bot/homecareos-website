Every home care agency loses carers. Some move away, some go back to study, some leave care altogether, and some go to the agency down the road. When a carer resigns, the natural reaction is to get straight on with filling the gap in the rota. But the person leaving knows things about your agency that the people staying may not tell you.

An exit interview is a short, private conversation with a carer who is leaving, to find out why they are going and what you could do better. Done well and done consistently, exit interviews show you the patterns behind your turnover. Done badly, or left to whoever remembers, they produce a pile of forms that nobody reads.

This guide covers why exit interviews are worth doing, how to set them up, the questions to ask, what to do with the answers, and the duties that apply whenever a carer leaves.

## Why exit interviews matter in home care

Acas, in its guidance on [responding to an employee's resignation](https://www.acas.org.uk/resignation/responding-to-an-employees-resignation), says arranging an exit interview can be useful to understand why the employee is leaving, and that their reasons can help inform how you recruit or retain staff. It gives a simple example: if people regularly leave because the job is not what they expected, you could update future job adverts to be clearer about the role.

That example fits home care well. Carers who leave in their first weeks often say the work, the travel or the hours were not what they pictured. Our guides to [writing a care worker job advert](/blog/care-worker-job-advert.html) and [onboarding new carers](/blog/onboarding-new-carers-home-care.html) cover how to set expectations honestly from the start, but you only know whether you have got it right if you ask the people who leave.

Acas also says exit interviews can help you find out whether someone left because they felt excluded or held back in some way, deal with outstanding matters such as handing over work, and give you useful feedback on what you could change. Its page on [when employees feel excluded](https://www.acas.org.uk/improving-equality-diversity-and-inclusion/if-employees-do-not-feel-included) makes the same point.

Skills for Care's [top tips for adult social care workforce retention](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/Recruitment-support/Recruitment-planning/recruitment-guide.pdf) recommend using appraisals, exit interviews and staff surveys together to understand what motivates staff and what makes them more likely to stay or leave at different stages of their employment. Exit interviews are one source among several, not the whole picture.

## Know their limits

It is worth being honest about what exit interviews can and cannot do. Skills for Care's [recruitment and retention pilots final report](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/About-us/Recruitment-and-retention-pilots-final-report.pdf) found that providers widely used, and most often rated as very effective, actions such as regular supervision or one-to-ones, wellbeing support, flexible working and staff recognition. In contrast, exit interviews and leaver feedback were less consistently rated as effective. The report suggests that preventative and relational approaches play a more important role in retention than reactive measures.

By the time someone is in an exit interview, they have usually already decided. So treat exit interviews as a way to spot patterns across many leavers, and pair them with [stay interviews](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html), [supervision and appraisal](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html) and [employee engagement](/blog/employee-engagement-home-care.html) work that reaches carers while they are still with you.

The Department for Education's employer standards for social workers make a similar warning in a page called [Make exit interviews count](https://www.support-for-social-workers.education.gov.uk/employer-standards/standard-2/exit-interviews). It says many organisations either fail to do exit interviews, collect data without analysing it, or analyse it without taking any action. That guidance is written for children's social work employers, but the lesson applies to any care employer.

## Setting up an exit interview process

Make exit interviews part of your leaving procedure, not a favour you remember to ask for. Decide in advance:

- **Who is offered one.** Offer it to everyone who leaves, including carers who leave during probation or after only a few weeks. Early leavers are often the group you learn most from.
- **That it is voluntary.** There is no legal requirement for a carer to take part. Say clearly that it is optional and that their answers will not affect their reference.
- **When it happens.** During the notice period, close to the leaving date. The DfE guidance suggests scheduling it as close to the person's departure as possible. Avoid the last few minutes of the final shift, when it feels like one more form.
- **Who holds it.** Ideally not the carer's direct line manager, who may be part of the reason they are leaving. In a small agency that could be the registered manager, the owner or a senior from another team.
- **How it happens.** A face-to-face or phone conversation usually draws out more than a form. The DfE guidance advises against online questionnaires with limited response options, because they miss the detail.
- **Where answers are recorded and who can see them.** Keep notes factual, store them securely with restricted access, and decide how long you keep them. The ICO's [employment practices guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/employment/) covers handling workers' personal information under UK GDPR.
- **What happens if something serious comes up.** If a carer raises a safeguarding concern, a concern about a colleague, or bullying or discrimination, it must go into your normal safeguarding or grievance process, not stay in an exit form.

The DfE guidance also suggests giving people the questions in advance, using a consistent set of questions so answers can be compared, and keeping questions open and non-judgemental.

## Care worker exit interview questions

Use the same core questions every time, so that after a few months you can see patterns. Pick from these, keep it to around a dozen, and leave space for anything else they want to say.

**The decision to leave**

1. What made you start thinking about leaving?
2. Was there a particular moment when you decided?
3. Is there anything we could have done that would have made you stay?
4. Where are you going next, if you are happy to say? Is it another care role, or a different kind of work?

**The job itself**

5. Was the job what you expected when you applied? If not, what was different?
6. How did you find your rota: the hours, the notice you were given about changes, and how fairly visits were shared out?
7. How manageable was the travel between visits, and did you feel it was paid fairly?
8. Did you have enough time at each visit to give the care in the care plan?

**Support and management**

9. How well did your induction and shadowing prepare you for visiting people on your own?
10. How supported did you feel by the office and your manager, including out of hours?
11. Did you have regular supervision, and was it useful to you?
12. Did you feel your work was noticed and appreciated?

**Culture and inclusion**

13. Did you ever feel excluded, held back or treated unfairly at work?
14. How well did the team communicate with each other and with you?

**Looking forward**

15. What did you enjoy most about working here?
16. What one thing would you change for the carers who are staying?
17. Would you consider coming back in the future?

The DfE guidance lists topics worth covering: job satisfaction, workloads and support; experiences with supervisors and leadership; experiences of inclusion or discrimination; workplace culture and team dynamics; career development; and work-life balance. The questions above map onto those topics, with rota, travel and visit time added because they matter so much in home care. Skills for Care's guidance on [staff wellbeing, rewards and recognition](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Staff-wellbeing-rewards-and-recognition.aspx) notes that inconsistent shifts or uncertain income make staff more likely to feel dissatisfied and consider leaving.

## Running the conversation

A few habits make the difference between polite answers and honest ones:

- **Explain why you are asking.** Say you want to understand their experience and improve things for the carers who stay, and that positive and negative feedback are both welcome.
- **Listen, do not defend.** If a carer says the rota was unfair, the exit interview is not the place to argue that it was not. Thank them, ask for an example and write it down.
- **Ask for examples.** "Can you tell me about a time when that happened?" turns a general complaint into something you can act on.
- **Keep it short and private.** Find a quiet place or a time to call when they are not between visits.
- **Record their words, not your interpretation.** Note the main reasons in the carer's own words, then add a short summary category for analysis, such as rota, pay, travel, management, workload, personal reasons or career change.
- **Close well.** Thank them, confirm anything outstanding such as final pay, holiday pay, returning equipment or ID badges, and how to contact you for a reference.

You can also ask the people a carer has supported. Skills for Care's guide on [involving people who use care in recruitment and retention](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/Recruitment-support/Application-and-selection-process/How-to-involve-people-who-use-care-in-recruitment-and-retention.pdf) suggests asking people who need care and support what they will miss about the person who is leaving, and what they want the new person to be like in terms of values, behaviours and attitudes. That gives you something useful for your next advert and interview.

## Turning answers into action

One exit interview is a story. Twenty are evidence. Every month or quarter:

1. **Group the reasons.** Count leavers by your summary categories and by length of service, for example under three months, three to twelve months and over a year.
2. **Look for patterns.** Are early leavers saying the job was not what they expected? Are carers in one area or on one run saying travel is unworkable? Do several people mention the same office process or the same weekend pattern?
3. **Compare with your turnover figures.** Our guide to [reducing carer turnover](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html) shows how to calculate turnover consistently, so you can see whether the patterns are getting better or worse.
4. **Pick one or two changes.** Choose changes you can actually make, such as clearer adverts, a longer shadowing period, a fairer way of sharing weekend visits, or a named contact for new starters.
5. **Tell the team.** Let the carers who are staying know what you heard and what you are changing. It shows that feedback is taken seriously, which makes stay interviews and surveys more honest too.
6. **Check whether it worked.** Look at the same categories again in three to six months.

Our guide to [care worker retention ideas](/blog/care-worker-retention-ideas.html) sets out what Skills for Care's national workforce data shows about who stays and who leaves, which can help you decide where to start.

## Duties when a carer leaves

An exit interview is optional. Some steps when a carer leaves are not. Skills for Care's guidance on [ending employment safely and responsibly](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Safer-recruitment/Ending-employment-safely-and-responsibly.aspx) says employers should follow a clear leaving process for all staff, record the reason for leaving including any safeguarding or conduct concerns, provide factual and accurate references when requested, consider whether a referral to the DBS or another regulatory body is required, and keep clear records of decisions, actions and information shared.

On DBS referrals, GOV.UK's guidance on [making barring referrals to the DBS](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/making-barring-referrals-to-the-dbs) says a regulated activity provider must refer when two conditions are met: it withdraws permission for someone to engage in regulated activity (or would have done), and it thinks the person has harmed or put a child or vulnerable adult at risk of harm, satisfied the harm test, or received a relevant caution or conviction. The guidance makes clear this includes situations where you would have taken that action but the person resigned or left. A carer resigning during an investigation does not end your duty.

For references, see our guide to [care worker references](/blog/care-worker-references.html) and Acas's guidance on [giving references](https://www.acas.org.uk/references). Keep what you say factual and consistent with your records.

## A simple exit interview checklist

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Resignation received | Acknowledge it in writing, confirm the last day and final pay, and offer an exit interview |
| Before the interview | Agree who will hold it, share the questions in advance and book a private time |
| The interview | Explain the purpose, ask the same open questions, listen and record their words |
| Serious concerns | Move any safeguarding, conduct, bullying or discrimination issue into the right process straight away |
| After they leave | Record the reason for leaving, consider any DBS referral, and store notes securely |
| Every month or quarter | Group reasons by category and length of service, choose changes and tell the team |
