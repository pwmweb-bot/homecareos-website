In most industries, a member of staff off sick means work waits until tomorrow. In home care it means a person is waiting at home for their breakfast, their medicines or help to get out of bed. That is why sickness absence feels so urgent in a care agency, and why it is so easy to handle badly.

Handle it too loosely and the same few carers keep picking up the extra calls until they burn out too. Handle it too harshly and carers come to work when they should not, which in home care can mean carrying an infection into the homes of older and unwell people. UKHSA's [staff health guidance for adult social care](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/infection-prevention-and-control-ipc-in-adult-social-care-asc/staff-health-and-managing-sickness-related-to-infection-in-adult-social-care) makes the link directly: in homecare services, staff absence can lead to delayed or missed visits if cover is not available, and supporting wellbeing helps reduce sickness and keep care consistent.

This guide is for registered managers and coordinators. It covers what the law says, what UKHSA and Acas advise, and a simple process you can use. It is general guidance, not legal advice. For individual cases, the [Acas helpline](https://www.acas.org.uk/managing-absence-and-returning-to-work) is a good first call.

## Start with a written absence policy

Acas's guide to [creating absence policies](https://www.acas.org.uk/absence-from-work) says a policy helps everyone understand their responsibilities and helps employers manage absence consistently. It suggests covering:

- how workers should report unplanned absence
- how you handle unauthorised absence and lateness
- whether you use a trigger point system and how it works
- how workers should keep in touch while they are off
- the support available while they are off and when they return
- what to do if someone needs time off for reasons related to a disability
- what you will pay during absence

For a home care agency, add the details that matter at 6.30am:

- **Who to call, and by when.** Give one number that is answered from early morning, and a cut-off time before the first call. A text to a colleague is not a report.
- **What the carer should tell you.** Whether they are fit to finish today's calls, any calls already done, and anything the next carer needs to know.
- **Infection rules.** When a carer must stay away from work, in plain words (see below).
- **Who arranges cover.** Carers should never be left to swap calls among themselves without the office knowing.

Go through the policy at induction and again at supervision, not just in the staff handbook. Our guide to [onboarding new carers](/blog/onboarding-new-carers-home-care.html) covers where it fits in the first weeks.

## Sick pay: the April 2026 changes

Sick pay is the part many agencies still have out of date. Acas's page on [Statutory Sick Pay](https://www.acas.org.uk/checking-sick-pay/statutory-sick-pay-ssp) says the eligibility rules changed on **6 April 2026**. Workers no longer need to:

- earn a minimum amount (the lower earnings limit), or
- be off sick for at least 4 days in a row (the "waiting days").

So an eligible carer is now entitled to SSP from the first full day of sickness. GOV.UK's [employer guide to SSP](https://www.gov.uk/employers-sick-pay) says it is **£123.25 a week or 80% of average weekly earnings, whichever is lower**, paid for up to 28 weeks. Average weekly earnings are worked out over the 8 weeks before the absence.

Three points catch care agencies out:

- **Qualifying days for irregular hours.** SSP is paid for the days the carer would have worked. Acas says that if someone has no regular pattern, you should agree with them which days count as qualifying days. If there is no agreement, any day they could be required to work may count. With rotas that change week to week, agree this in writing.
- **Part days.** Acas's guidance on [phased returns](https://www.acas.org.uk/returning-to-work-after-absence/phased-return-to-work) says SSP can only be paid for full non-working days. A carer who does the morning run and goes home ill is paid for the calls they worked, not SSP for the rest of the day.
- **Holiday keeps building up.** GOV.UK says statutory annual leave is accrued while an employee is off sick, however long they are off.

If you pay more than SSP through a company scheme, it must be written into the contract. Check your payroll provider has applied the April 2026 rules, especially for part-time carers who previously earned too little to qualify.

## Fit notes and self-certification

GOV.UK's page on [taking sick leave](https://www.gov.uk/taking-sick-leave) sets out the evidence rules:

- For **7 days or less**, carers can self-certify. They do not need a fit note.
- For **more than 7 days in a row**, including weekends and bank holidays, they must give you a fit note.
- A fit note can come from a GP, hospital doctor, registered nurse, occupational therapist, pharmacist or physiotherapist. It can be printed or digital.

If the fit note says the carer **may be fit for work**, GOV.UK says you should discuss changes that might help them return, such as different hours or tasks. In home care that could mean shorter runs, no double-up moving and handling calls for a while, or calls closer to home. If you cannot agree the changes, the carer must be treated as not fit for work.

## When carers must stay away: infection

This is where home care differs from most workplaces. A carer with a stomach bug can pass it to several people in one shift, each of them in their own home. UKHSA's resource on [staff health and managing sickness related to infection](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/infection-prevention-and-control-ipc-in-adult-social-care-asc/staff-health-and-managing-sickness-related-to-infection-in-adult-social-care), published in October 2026, says staff should not work when infectious, and gives minimum exclusion periods. Some of the common ones:

| Condition | UKHSA advice for care and support workers |
|---|---|
| Diarrhoea and/or vomiting | Stay away until clinically well and 48 hours without diarrhoea or vomiting |
| Acute respiratory infection | Stay away while they have a high temperature or are not well enough to work. Mild symptoms only, such as a runny nose, usually do not need exclusion if they follow standard precautions |
| Chickenpox | Until all blisters are dry and no new ones are forming |
| Shingles | Only if the rash is weeping and cannot be safely covered |
| Unexplained rash | Until assessed by a healthcare professional |

Check the full table before you rely on it, and during an outbreak follow advice from your local UKHSA Health Protection Team. The same guidance says registered managers should:

- support staff to report symptoms and stay away from work when needed
- have risk assessments for staff who are more vulnerable to infection, for example those who are pregnant or have a weakened immune system
- have plans to make sure care can continue when staff are off with an infection

Make the 48-hour rule easy to follow. If carers lose pay or feel judged for staying off, some will come in. Day-one SSP helps, and so does a manager who says "thank you for telling us" rather than "who is going to cover this?". Our guide to [infection prevention and control in home care](/blog/infection-prevention-control-home-care.html) covers the wider duties.

## Keeping in touch while someone is off

Acas's page on [sick leave](https://www.acas.org.uk/sick-leave) says employers can contact someone who is off sick, and that the right amount of contact depends on their circumstances. Agree it with the carer rather than ringing every day. For longer absences, a short weekly check-in is usually enough to show they have not been forgotten.

Acas also says employers must treat mental and physical health as equally important, and that if someone is off with a mental health problem you should look at whether work played a part. In home care, that might be a difficult client, a run with no breaks, or something they saw on a call. Our guide to [care worker wellbeing](/blog/care-worker-wellbeing-home-care.html) covers stress and support in more depth.

## Return to work meetings

Acas's guide to [return to work meetings](https://www.acas.org.uk/returning-to-work-after-absence) says they are not a legal requirement, but a chat as soon as possible after someone returns is a good chance to:

- make sure they are ready to come back
- update them on anything that changed while they were off
- see if they need any support
- agree a plan, such as a phased return
- agree what colleagues should know and what stays confidential

For a carer, "what changed" often matters more than in an office job. Tell them about new clients on their run, care plan changes, medication changes and anything the family raised. Acas has a free [return to work meeting template](https://www.acas.org.uk/return-to-work-meeting-template) you can adapt.

Keep it short and supportive. A return to work meeting that feels like an interrogation teaches carers to hide the real reason next time.

## Phased returns

After a long illness or injury, Acas suggests a [phased return](https://www.acas.org.uk/returning-to-work-after-absence/phased-return-to-work): reduced hours, different duties or a lighter workload, agreed together and reviewed regularly. A fit note will often give advice on how long it should last.

In home care, a phased return might look like:

- morning calls only for the first two weeks
- single-handed calls with lighter moving and handling needs
- a run in a smaller area, with less driving
- shadowing a colleague on any new clients

If the carer is disabled, Acas says you must also make reasonable adjustments. Write down what you agreed, including pay, and set a date to review it.

## Patterns, trigger points and the Bradford Factor

Many agencies use trigger points to decide when to review someone's absence. Acas's page on [absence trigger points](https://www.acas.org.uk/absence-trigger-points) says there are no legal rules about how or when absence should be reviewed, and that a review point system can help. But it is clear on the limits:

- Set the review points out clearly in your absence policy.
- Reaching a review point should start an absence review, which is a conversation about wellbeing, the cause and support.
- **Do not use review points to trigger disciplinary action automatically.** Acas warns that if staff see the system as a punishment, it can cause more absence, for example people coming back too early.

Before you decide on any next step, Acas says to consider whether the absence might be disability-related, linked to pregnancy or mental health, caused by work, or covered by time off for dependants, and whether occupational health or more flexible working would help.

Some agencies use the **Bradford Factor** as a trigger. It squares the number of separate spells of absence and multiplies by the total days, so several short spells score much higher than one long one. It can be a useful early prompt to have a conversation. It should never be the decision. A carer with a long-term condition, or a pregnant carer, can score high for reasons the law protects.

## Look at the causes you control

Sickness absence is partly about the individual and partly about the job. UKHSA's guidance says registered managers should help create working conditions that support good health, including appropriate breaks, staying hydrated and managing workloads where possible.

Questions worth asking when absence goes up:

- Are some runs too tight, with no time to eat or drink?
- Are the same carers always picking up extra calls when others are off?
- Do split shifts and late bed calls leave enough rest before early starts? Our guide to [care worker shift patterns](/blog/care-worker-shift-patterns.html) covers the working time rules.
- Are carers getting regular [supervision](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html), where problems can come up before they turn into sick days?
- Are there enough carers on the books for the hours you have promised?

That last one is often the real issue. When an agency runs with no slack, every sick day turns into a crisis, the people covering get tired, and absence spreads. Steady recruitment is part of absence management. Our guides to [how to recruit care workers in the UK](/blog/how-to-recruit-care-workers-uk.html) and [reducing carer turnover](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html) cover both sides of that.

## A simple sickness absence process for a home care agency

1. **Report.** The carer calls the office or on-call number before their first call, using the number in your policy.
2. **Check infection.** If it sounds infectious, apply the UKHSA exclusion period and tell the carer when they can return.
3. **Cover.** The office takes the carer off their calls and arranges cover, starting with the most time-critical visits such as medication and personal care.
4. **Record.** Log the start date, reason category and expected return. Note whether a fit note will be needed after 7 days.
5. **Pay.** Pay SSP from the first full day for qualifying days, or your company scheme if better.
6. **Keep in touch.** Agree how often you will speak, especially for longer absences.
7. **Return.** Hold a short return to work meeting, update them on changes to their clients, and agree any phased return.
8. **Review.** Look at patterns monthly. Use trigger points to start supportive conversations, not to issue automatic warnings.
