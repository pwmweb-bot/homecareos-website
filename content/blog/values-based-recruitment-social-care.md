Most home care agencies already know what they want in a carer. Someone kind, reliable and honest, who treats a person's home with respect and stays calm when a visit does not go to plan. The hard part is finding that out before you hire, not three weeks into a probation period.

Values-based recruitment is the structured way to do it. This guide explains what it is, how it fits with your safer recruitment duties, and how to use Skills for Care's five stages in a small or medium home care agency.

## What values-based recruitment means

Skills for Care's [values-based recruitment toolkit](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Values-based-recruitment.aspx) describes it as an evidence-based approach that helps employers understand a candidate's values, behaviours and attitudes, and assess whether they fit the values, culture and expectations of the workplace.

The idea is simple. Rather than hiring only on experience, a driving licence and availability, you also look for the behaviours that lead to compassionate, person-centred care. Skills for Care says employers who use the approach have reported lower recruitment costs, better retention and better performance.

For domiciliary care it matters even more. A new carer will often work alone in someone's home, with no colleague in the next room. How they behave when nobody is watching is the job.

## How it fits with safer recruitment

Values-based recruitment does not replace your legal checks. CQC's guidance on [Regulation 19: fit and proper persons employed](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-19) requires the people you employ to be of good character and to have the qualifications, competence, skills and experience the work needs, and it expects you to hold the information listed in Schedule 3.

That still means identity, a DBS check at the right level (GOV.UK's [DBS eligibility tool](https://www.gov.uk/find-out-dbs-check) helps you confirm it), references, a full employment history with gaps explained, and [right to work checks](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/right-to-work-checks-employers-guide) done the way the Home Office sets out.

Think of it as two questions. Safer recruitment asks: is it safe to employ this person? Values-based recruitment asks: is this the right person for the people we support? You need a yes to both. Our guide to [safer recruitment in domiciliary care](/blog/safer-recruitment-domiciliary-care.html) and the [care worker recruitment checklist](/blog/care-worker-recruitment-checklist.html) cover the first question in detail.

## The five stages, for a home care agency

Skills for Care's toolkit follows five stages. Here is what each one looks like in practice for a home care agency.

### 1. Articulate: decide what your values look like

Skills for Care's [Articulate](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Articulate.aspx) stage asks you to define your values and describe the behaviours that go with them. The behaviours are the useful part. "Respect" on a poster tells a candidate very little. "Knocks and waits, asks before moving things, and talks to the person, not over them" tells them exactly what you mean.

A practical way to start:

- Pick four or five values. Involve your carers, and if you can, the people you support and their families, as Skills for Care suggests.
- For each value, write two or three behaviours you would expect to see on a visit.
- Write one or two behaviours you would not accept, such as rushing a call to get to the next one, or leaving without recording what happened.

Keep this list short. You will use it in your adverts, your interview scoring and your supervision, so it has to be easy to remember.

### 2. Attract: show your values in your adverts

The [Attract](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Attract.aspx) stage is about reaching people who share your values, including people who have not worked in care before. Skills for Care names career changers, people returning to work, older or retired workers, people with transferable skills, people from different communities and neurodivergent candidates.

In your advert:

- Say what your values are, in plain English, with one real example of what they look like on a visit.
- Be clear about pay, hours, travel and what the job involves, so people can rule themselves in or out.
- Say whether experience is essential. If you train new starters, say so.
- Add a line about reasonable adjustments. Skills for Care suggests telling applicants you will support disabled and neurodivergent candidates who need adjustments.

Your existing carers are often your best route to people who share your values. Skills for Care points to staff referral schemes such as Care Friends. Our guide to [writing a care worker job advert](/blog/care-worker-job-advert.html) has a full structure you can copy.

### 3. Apply: let applicants show their values

The [Apply](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Apply.aspx) stage is about designing an application that lets candidates show their values and motivation, not just list past jobs.

Keep it short and easy to finish on a phone. Long forms lose good applicants. Two or three open questions are enough, for example:

- Tell us about a time you helped someone who was struggling. What did you do?
- Why do you want to work in home care?
- What would you do if you arrived at a visit and the person did not want your help today?

Life experience counts. Skills for Care notes that candidates may show their values through things outside paid work, such as caring for a family member or volunteering. Make it clear in the form that those answers are welcome.

### 4. Assess: score values, not impressions

The [Assess](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Assess.aspx) stage recommends using a range of methods: values-based interviews, scenario discussions, work observations, practical exercises, meeting the team or the people who draw on care, and written or online exercises. It also asks you to link your assessment criteria to your values and to assess every candidate fairly and consistently.

For a home care agency, a workable approach is:

- **A short values interview** with the same questions for every candidate, scored against the behaviours you wrote at stage 1. Our list of [care worker interview questions](/blog/care-worker-interview-questions.html) includes values and scenario questions.
- **One or two home care scenarios**, such as a missed medication, a person who seems unwell, or a family member who asks the carer to do something outside the care plan.
- **A second opinion.** If you can, have an experienced carer join part of the interview, or ask a person you support or their family what they would want in a carer.

Score each answer straight after the interview and keep the notes. They help you make a fair decision, and they show how you reached it.

Skills for Care also suggests ways to make assessment neuro-inclusive, such as sending questions in advance, giving written copies of questions during the interview and letting candidates use notes.

### 5. Assimilate: keep values alive after the offer

The [Assimilate](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Assimilate.aspx) stage is about supporting new staff through induction, supervision and ongoing development, so they understand your expectations and put your values into practice.

This is where many agencies lose the benefit. A carer hired for their values can still leave quickly if their first weeks are chaotic. In practice:

- Use your values in induction and shadow shifts, with experienced carers showing what each one looks like.
- Bring the values into [supervision and appraisal](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html), so feedback talks about behaviours, not only tasks completed.
- Recognise carers when they show your values, and say which one.

Our guides to [onboarding new carers](/blog/onboarding-new-carers-home-care.html) and [care worker retention](/blog/care-worker-retention-ideas.html) go further on the first weeks and beyond.

## Common mistakes

- **Values on the wall, not in the process.** If your interview never asks about your values, candidates will not take them seriously either.
- **Gut feel instead of scoring.** "I liked her" is not evidence. Score against written behaviours.
- **Letting values excuse missing checks.** A great interview never replaces a DBS check, references or a right to work check.
- **Slow decisions.** Good candidates take other jobs while they wait. Move people who score well on to checks quickly.
- **Forgetting it after day one.** Values that never come up again in supervision fade fast.
