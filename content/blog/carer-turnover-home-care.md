Ask a registered manager how turnover is going and you will usually get a feeling rather than a number. "Bad this month." "Better since the summer." That feeling is often right, but it does not tell you who is leaving, when, or whether the things you changed made any difference.

This guide shows how to calculate carer turnover in a way you can compare month to month and against national data, how to find out why people go, and which changes in recognition, rotas and communication are most worth trying. For the wider list of retention ideas, see our guide to [care worker retention ideas that actually work](/blog/care-worker-retention-ideas.html).

## Why turnover matters in home care

Every carer who leaves takes runs, client relationships and local knowledge with them. Skills for Care's [summary of domiciliary care services for 2025/26](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Adult-Social-Care-Workforce-Data/workforceintelligence/resources/Reports/National/Summary-of-domiciliary-care-services-2026.pdf) puts turnover in CQC-regulated non-residential services at **23.5%**, and **28.6% for care workers**, around 93,000 care worker leavers in a year. Nearly half (48%) of recruitment in those services comes from within adult social care, so many of the carers you lose are going to another provider, not leaving care.

Skills for Care's [2026 workforce report](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Adult-Social-Care-Workforce-Data/workforceintelligence/Reports-and-visualisations/National-information/The-State-of-report.aspx) also notes that the high level of movement in the workforce may affect service delivery and continuity of care. For a home care agency, that is the client meeting yet another new face at the door.

The same report has a more hopeful figure: around **23% of independent sector employers** with 50 or more staff had a turnover rate **below 10%**. High turnover is common, but it is not a law of nature.

## How to calculate carer turnover

The standard method, used by HR bodies such as the [CIPD](https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/factsheets/turnover-retention-factsheet/), is:

**Turnover rate = (leavers in the period ÷ average number of staff employed in the period) × 100**

To make the number useful, decide a few rules once and stick to them:

- **Period.** Use a rolling 12 months for your headline figure, so one bad month does not swing it. Look at monthly leavers too.
- **Who counts as staff.** Skills for Care's national turnover figures cover **directly employed staff only** (permanent and temporary). Leavers from agency roles are not included. If you want to compare with them, do the same.
- **Who counts as a leaver.** Count everyone who left your employment: resignations, dismissals, retirements and people who never came back after a few shifts. Do not count internal moves, such as a carer becoming a field supervisor.
- **Average headcount.** The simplest version is your headcount at the start of the period plus your headcount at the end, divided by two. If you can, average the headcount at the end of each month instead, which copes better with growth.

### A worked example

The figures below are **example figures only**, to show the sum.

An agency had 40 carers on 1 November last year and 46 carers on 31 October this year. Over those 12 months, 13 carers left.

- Average headcount: (40 + 46) ÷ 2 = 43
- Turnover rate: 13 ÷ 43 × 100 = **30.2%**

That would be above the 28.6% national figure for care workers in non-residential services. On its own, it does not tell you what to fix. The next measures do.

## Look beneath the headline number

The headline rate hides the pattern. Break it down every month into the few measures below.

### Early leavers

Skills for Care's analysis of [factors affecting turnover](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Adult-Social-Care-Workforce-Data/workforceintelligence/Reports-and-visualisations/Topics/Factors-affecting-turnover.aspx) found that care workers who had been in their role for **less than a year** had a turnover rate of **33.7%**, against 12.6% for those with 20 years or more. In home care, the first year is where the risk sits.

Count leavers who went within **3, 6 and 12 months** of starting. If most of your leavers are in the first three months, look at your advert, interview and induction before you look at pay. Our guides to [writing a care worker job advert](/blog/care-worker-job-advert.html), [care worker interview questions](/blog/care-worker-interview-questions.html) and [onboarding new carers](/blog/onboarding-new-carers-home-care.html) cover those stages.

### Stability

A stability rate shows how much of your experienced team you are keeping. One common version is:

**Stability = (staff with a year or more of service ÷ staff in post a year ago) × 100**

If turnover is high but stability is also high, you are mostly losing new starters and keeping your core team. If both are bad, the problem is wider.

### Voluntary and involuntary leavers

Split resignations from dismissals and people who left because their probation did not work out. A rise in dismissals points at recruitment and induction. A rise in resignations points at the job itself.

### Leavers by team, area or manager

If you run more than one area or office, calculate turnover for each. Skills for Care's 2026 report found care worker turnover of 28.3% where the registered manager had been in post for less than a year, against 20.6% where they had been in post 20 years or more. Local leadership matters, and the breakdown shows you where.

### Sickness absence

Sickness often rises before someone resigns. Our guide to the [Bradford Factor in home care](/blog/bradford-factor-home-care-uk-guide.html) explains one way to track short, frequent absences, and why it needs to be used fairly.

## Work out what each leaver costs you

You do not need a national figure to make the case for acting. You can cost your own leavers. For a typical carer who leaves, add up:

- **Advertising**: job board fees and boosted posts
- **Admin time**: shortlisting, interviews, chasing references and DBS
- **Induction and training**: the Care Certificate, mandatory training and paid shadow shifts
- **Cover**: overtime, extra mileage and any agency staff while the post is empty
- **Management time**: rota changes, client calls and sorting handed-back visits
- **Lost work**: care packages you had to turn down because you did not have the hours

Use your own invoices and payroll. Once you have a cost per leaver, compare it with the cost of the changes below, such as guaranteed hours or a better induction. That turns a feeling into a business decision.

## Find out why carers leave

### Exit interviews

Offer every leaver a short exit conversation, ideally with someone other than their direct line manager. Skills for Care's [top tips for workforce retention](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/Recruitment-support/Recruitment-planning/recruitment-guide.pdf) recommends using appraisals, exit interviews and staff surveys to understand what makes staff more likely to stay or leave at different stages of their employment.

Keep the questions the same each time so you can count the answers:

- What made you decide to leave?
- Was the job what you expected when you started?
- Was there a point when you started thinking about leaving? What happened?
- How did you find your rota, runs and travel time?
- Did you feel supported when things were difficult?
- What could we have done that would have made you stay?

Skills for Care's guide on [involving people who use care in recruitment and retention](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/Recruitment-support/Application-and-selection-process/How-to-involve-people-who-use-care-in-recruitment-and-retention.pdf) also suggests asking the people you support what they will miss about a carer who is leaving, and what they want from the next one.

### Stay interviews

An exit interview tells you about a problem after it has cost you a carer. A stay interview asks your current team before they decide to go. NHS North West London's [Keeping Well guidance on exit and stay interviews](https://www.keepingwellnwl.nhs.uk/academy/using-workforce-intelligence-data/exit-interviews) suggests keeping it informal, private and under 20 minutes. Useful questions include:

- What keeps you working here?
- What is the hardest part of your week?
- If you could change one thing about your job, what would it be?
- Is there anything that might make you think about leaving?
- What can I do to support you better?

The value is in what happens next. If a carer tells you their Tuesday run is impossible, and it is the same run in a month, the conversation has made things worse.

### What the research says carers leave over

In a 2023 Skills for Care scoping study, [Understanding the reasons care workers move on](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/Recruitment-support/Retaining-your-staff/Understanding-the-reasons-care-workers-move-on-and-their-future-intentions.pdf), 539 current care workers in two regions were asked about their last care job. Almost a third (**30%**) said they left because of **poor workplace culture or communication**, a fifth (**20%**) cited **burnout or stress**, and **16%** said **low pay**. The most common motivations to stay were enjoying the work with clients (69%) and getting on well with colleagues (65%). Only 21% said they stayed because they were happy with the pay.

The researchers are clear that the sample was small and self-selecting, so the findings are indicative rather than representative. Pay was also the top reason among those who were thinking of leaving their current job (25%). The point is not that pay does not matter. It is that culture, communication and workload sit alongside it, and those are things a manager can change this month.

## Three levers that reduce carer turnover

Once you know where and why carers are leaving, these three areas are where most agencies can act quickly. They are covered in more depth in our [retention ideas guide](/blog/care-worker-retention-ideas.html).

### 1. Recognition that is specific and frequent

Skills for Care's guidance on [staff wellbeing, rewards and recognition](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Staff-wellbeing-rewards-and-recognition.aspx) says everyday actions that show staff they are valued can make a real difference in helping people choose to stay. In home care, where carers rarely see the office, that means:

- passing on a compliment from a client or family to the carer by name, the same day
- thanking people who pick up short-notice calls, and making sure it is not always the same few
- marking three-month, six-month and one-year milestones, given how many people leave in year one

### 2. Rotas that feel fair

The same Skills for Care guidance links inconsistent shifts and uncertain income with dissatisfaction, and recommends applying rotas, workloads, pay and overtime consistently. Fairness is partly about the rota and partly about whether carers can see it is fair:

- publish rotas as early as you can and keep late changes to a minimum
- share weekends, bank holidays and difficult calls evenly, and be able to show you did
- build runs with realistic travel time, and pay it lawfully (see our guide to [national minimum wage and travel time](/blog/national-minimum-wage-travel-time-home-care.html))
- ask each carer what hours they want, and review it when your client base changes

Our guide to [how to build a home care rota](/blog/how-to-build-a-home-care-rota.html) goes into run design.

### 3. Communication that closes the loop

If poor communication is one of the most common reasons carers give for leaving, it deserves the same attention as pay. Practical steps:

- explain **why** a rota or client change was made, not only what changed
- make sure carers know who to call out of hours, and that someone answers
- share what you changed after a staff survey or stay interview, even if it is small
- use [supervision](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html) to ask how the carer is, not only to check paperwork, and check in more often during the first few months

Skills for Care's [Building a workforce that stays](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Building-a-workforce-that-stays.aspx) guidance makes the same point: people rarely leave or stay for one reason, so a joined-up approach is more likely to last than a one-off initiative.

## Benchmark and review every month

A simple monthly routine is enough for most agencies:

1. Update your rolling 12-month turnover and stability figures.
2. List the month's leavers with their length of service and reason for leaving.
3. Note any themes from exit and stay interviews.
4. Pick one change to make, and check next month whether it moved the numbers.

If you keep your records in Skills for Care's Adult Social Care Workforce Data Set (ASC-WDS), its 2026 report says you can compare your turnover, vacancy rate, pay, sickness and qualifications with other employers in your local area using its benchmarking tools. The monthly [recruitment and retention tracker](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Adult-Social-Care-Workforce-Data/workforceintelligence/Monthly-statistics/Recruitment-and-retention.aspx) gives a wider national picture.
