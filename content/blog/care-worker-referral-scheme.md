Ask a registered manager where their best carers came from and you will often hear the same answer: "Someone here recommended them." People who already do the job know what it involves, and they know who in their life has the patience, reliability and kindness it needs.

A refer-a-friend scheme turns that into a habit rather than a happy accident. Done well, it brings in applicants who understand the role before they start. Done badly, it can create tax surprises, resentment over unpaid rewards, a workforce that all looks the same, and the temptation to wave a friend's friend through without proper checks.

This guide is for registered managers, recruitment coordinators and owners of home care agencies in England. It sits alongside our guides on [how to recruit care workers in the UK](/blog/how-to-recruit-care-workers-uk.html), [writing a care worker job advert](/blog/care-worker-job-advert.html) and [values-based recruitment](/blog/values-based-recruitment-social-care.html). It is not legal, tax or HR advice.

## Why referrals suit home care

Skills for Care's [Attract](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Values-based-recruitment/Attract.aspx) guidance for values-based recruitment says your existing workforce can be one of your strongest recruitment channels, and encourages employers to ask staff to recommend people who share their values and understand the role, through referral schemes.

That fits domiciliary care particularly well:

- **Carers know the reality.** They can tell a friend honestly about early starts, driving between visits, lone working and what a good day feels like. That helps people rule themselves in or out before they apply.
- **Local reach.** Your carers live in the areas you cover. Their friends are likely to live nearby too, which matters when every visit involves travel.
- **Belonging from day one.** A new starter who already knows someone in the team has a familiar face to ask the questions they would not ask a manager.
- **A signal of engagement.** Carers rarely recommend a place they would not want a friend to work. If nobody refers anyone, that tells you something too. Our guide to [employee engagement in home care](/blog/employee-engagement-home-care.html) suggests tracking how many applicants your own carers refer.

## Rule one: a referral is not a shortcut

Every person you employ to provide a regulated activity must meet [Regulation 19](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-19), the fit and proper persons employed duty, and you must hold the information listed in [Schedule 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/2936/schedule/3). It makes no difference whether they came through a job board, a leaflet or a colleague.

So a referred applicant goes through exactly the same steps as anyone else:

- an application and interview, assessed against the same criteria
- a [right to work check](https://www.gov.uk/check-job-applicant-right-to-work) before they start
- an appropriate [DBS check](/blog/dbs-check-for-care-workers.html)
- [references](/blog/care-worker-references.html), including evidence of conduct in previous health or social care work and why it ended
- the health information Schedule 3 asks for, gathered fairly after a conditional offer, as in our [pre-employment health questionnaire](/blog/pre-employment-health-questionnaire-care-workers.html) guide

Two points are worth putting in writing. First, the referring carer should not act as the applicant's referee: a friend's opinion is not evidence of conduct in previous employment. Second, the referring carer should not sit on the interview panel or make the hiring decision. Our [care worker recruitment checklist](/blog/care-worker-recruitment-checklist.html) and [safer recruitment in domiciliary care](/blog/safer-recruitment-domiciliary-care.html) guides set out the full process.

Tell staff this up front. It protects them too. Nobody wants to be the person who vouched for someone who was later found unsuitable.

## Designing the reward

There is no right amount, and this guide does not suggest one. What matters more is that the scheme is clear, fair and paid reliably. Decide and write down:

- **Who can refer.** All employees, or only care staff? Office staff and managers often have strong networks too. Decide whether people involved in hiring decisions can claim rewards.
- **Who counts as a referral.** Usually someone who is not already in your recruitment process or has not applied in the last few months. Agree how you will settle two staff referring the same person.
- **What the reward is.** Cash through payroll, extra paid time off, or a choice. Be careful with vouchers: see the tax section below.
- **When it is earned.** A single reward after probation links the reward to people who stay. Staged rewards, for example a thank you at start date and the rest after probation, keep the scheme visible. Our guide to the [care worker probation period](/blog/care-worker-probation-period.html) covers what passing probation should mean.
- **What happens if things change.** If the new carer leaves before the qualifying date, or the referrer leaves, does the reward still apply? Say so in advance to avoid disputes.
- **Recognition, not only money.** A thank you in a team message or at a staff meeting costs nothing. Our guide to [reducing carer turnover](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html) covers recognition that carers actually value.

## Tax and payroll

Referral rewards are not outside the tax system just because they are a thank you.

GOV.UK's guidance on [bonuses](https://www.gov.uk/expenses-and-benefits-bonuses/what-to-report-and-pay) says a cash bonus, including vouchers that can be exchanged for cash, counts as earnings, and that you may also have to use PAYE for non-cash bonuses that an employee can easily convert to cash.

You may have heard that small gifts are tax free. The [trivial benefits](https://www.gov.uk/expenses-and-benefits-trivial-benefits) exemption has conditions, including that the benefit is not cash or a cash voucher and is **not a reward for the employee's work or performance**. A reward for recommending someone you then hire is unlikely to fit comfortably, so do not rely on the exemption without advice. GOV.UK suggests calling the HMRC employer helpline if you are not sure.

In practice, the simplest approach is to pay referral rewards through payroll in the month they are earned, and agree the process with whoever runs your payroll before you launch.

## Keep it fair and diverse

Referrals tend to bring in people who are similar to your existing team. That can be a strength when it brings in people with the right values. It becomes a problem if it is the only way people hear about your jobs.

Acas, in its guidance on [advertising a job](https://www.acas.org.uk/recruitment/how-to-advertise-a-job), advises employers to consider whether the places they advertise reach a diverse range of people. Skills for Care's Attract page also talks about inclusive approaches that build a larger and more diverse talent pool.

So:

- **Always advertise openly as well.** Your website, job boards and local community channels should carry the same vacancies, as our [job advert guide](/blog/care-worker-job-advert.html) describes.
- **Give everyone the same information** about the role, pay and hours, however they heard about it.
- **Score everyone the same way.** Use the same interview questions and criteria for referred and non-referred applicants. Our [care worker interview questions](/blog/care-worker-interview-questions.html) guide can help.
- **Look at the pattern.** Every few months, compare where applicants and new starters came from. If referrals dominate, put more effort into other channels.

## Making it easy for carers to refer

Most referral schemes fail quietly because staff forget about them or find them awkward. A few habits help:

- **Give carers something to share.** A short message and a link to your vacancy or apply page they can send by text or social media. It is better for the friend to apply directly than for your carer to hand over someone's contact details without them knowing.
- **Mention it where carers already are.** Supervisions, team messages, staff meetings and induction. New starters often know other people looking for work.
- **Say thank you quickly.** Acknowledge every referral, even if the person is not hired, and tell the referrer when a reward is coming.
- **Pay on time.** Nothing kills a scheme faster than a reward that someone has to chase.
- **Record the source.** Note who referred each applicant on your own recruitment records so you can pay rewards accurately and see whether the scheme is working.

Some agencies use a dedicated referral app. Skills for Care announced a [partnership with Care Friends](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/news-and-events/news/skills-for-care-partners-with-employee-referral-app-care-friends), an employee referral app for social care, in 2020, and our [care recruitment software round-up](/blog/care-recruitment-software-uk.html) explains where a referral app fits alongside an applicant tracking system.

## A simple referral policy outline

1. **Purpose:** to encourage staff to recommend people who share our values for care roles.
2. **Eligibility:** who can refer, and who cannot claim (for example people making the hiring decision).
3. **Valid referral:** new to our recruitment process, applied through our normal route, referrer named at application.
4. **Process:** same advert, application, interview and safer recruitment checks as every other applicant. The referrer is not a referee and takes no part in the decision.
5. **Reward:** what it is, when it is earned, and what happens if either person leaves first.
6. **Payment:** through payroll, with tax and National Insurance applied as required.
7. **Fairness:** vacancies are always advertised openly as well; sources are reviewed regularly.
8. **Review:** the scheme is reviewed once a year, including feedback from staff.
