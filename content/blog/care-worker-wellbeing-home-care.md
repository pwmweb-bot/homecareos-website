Home care is rewarding work, and it is also hard work. Carers travel between visits in all weathers, work alone in other people's homes, support people at the end of their lives and often cover extra calls when a colleague is off. Skills for Care puts it simply on its page on [staff wellbeing, rewards and recognition](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Staff-wellbeing-rewards-and-recognition.aspx): adult social care roles can be physically and emotionally demanding, and without the right support staff may experience stress, burnout and reduced engagement.

Looking after carers' wellbeing is not a perk. It is part of what CQC assesses, part of your health and safety duties, and one of the most practical things you can do to keep the carers you already have.

This guide covers what the regulators expect, the everyday causes of stress in domiciliary care, how to spot when someone is struggling, and a simple wellbeing plan any home care agency can put in place.

## What CQC expects on staff wellbeing

CQC's single assessment framework includes a quality statement called [workforce wellbeing and enablement](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/assessment/single-assessment-framework/caring/workforce-wellbeing-enablement), under the Caring key question. The statement reads: "We care about and promote the wellbeing of our staff, and we support and enable them to always deliver person centred care."

CQC explains what that means in practice. Providers should recognise and meet the wellbeing needs of staff, including the resources and facilities for safe working, such as regular breaks. Staff should have regular opportunities to give feedback, raise concerns and suggest improvements, and leaders should respond in a timely and considered way. Staff who are struggling at work should be supported, and should have easy access to personalised support that recognises the diversity of the workforce. CQC also says people should be supported by staff who feel valued by their leaders and colleagues and have a sense of belonging.

Skills for Care's [inspection toolkit on workforce wellbeing and enablement](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Support-for-leaders-and-managers/Good-and-outstanding-care/inspection-toolkit/Topic-recommendations.aspx?kloe=caring-1&services=&topic=workforce-wellbeing-and-enablement) turns this into statements you can test your agency against, such as making sure staff have regular breaks, using supervision to discuss wellbeing and support needs, keeping wellbeing information confidential and secure, and reviewing your wellbeing support through staff surveys and acting on what you find.

## Your legal duties on stress and safety

Wellbeing also sits inside health and safety law. The HSE's guidance on [stress risk assessment](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/risk-assessment.htm) says employers have a legal duty to protect workers from stress at work by doing a risk assessment and acting on it, assessing stress in the same way as other work-related health and safety risks. If you have five or more workers, you must write the risk assessment down.

The HSE [Management Standards](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/) help you do this. They look at six areas of work design that can affect stress levels:

| Area | What it can look like in home care |
| --- | --- |
| Demands | Back-to-back calls with no travel time, too many visits on a run, last-minute extra calls |
| Control | Little say over rotas, areas or the clients a carer visits |
| Support | Hard to reach the office or on-call, no debrief after a difficult visit |
| Relationships | Unresolved conflict, bullying or unfair sharing of weekend and evening work |
| Role | Unclear expectations about tasks, or being asked to do things they are not trained for |
| Change | New systems, new clients or changed runs introduced without explanation |

Acas, in its guidance on [supporting mental health at work](https://www.acas.org.uk/supporting-mental-health-workplace), adds that employers have a duty of care to do all they reasonably can to support workers' health, safety and wellbeing, and that poor mental health should be treated as seriously and with the same care as a physical illness.

Lone working is part of the picture for almost every domiciliary carer. The HSE's guidance on [protecting lone workers](https://www.hse.gov.uk/lone-working/employer/index.htm) says employers must manage the health and safety risks before people work alone, and our guide to [lone working in home care](/blog/lone-working-home-care-agencies.html) covers how to do that.

## The everyday causes of stress in domiciliary care

Most stress in home care does not come from one dramatic event. It builds up from the way the work is organised. Common causes include:

- **Unrealistic runs.** Calls scheduled with no travel time, or visits that need longer than the time booked, leave carers rushing and running late all day. Our guide to [travel time and the minimum wage](/blog/national-minimum-wage-travel-time-home-care.html) covers the pay side of this too.
- **Unpredictable hours and income.** Skills for Care says that when shifts are inconsistent or income is uncertain, staff are more likely to feel dissatisfied and consider leaving.
- **Always covering gaps.** When the agency is short of staff, the same reliable carers get asked to pick up extra calls again and again.
- **Working alone with difficult situations.** Distressed or unwell clients, family conflict, safeguarding concerns or a client's death can stay with a carer long after the visit.
- **Feeling invisible.** Carers may go weeks without seeing a manager in person. If the only contact is a phone call about a missed or late visit, it is easy to feel unvalued.
- **Missed breaks.** Long split shifts can leave no real time to eat or rest.

## Know your carers' rights to rest

Rest is the foundation of wellbeing, and some of it is a legal right. GOV.UK's guide to [rest breaks at work](https://www.gov.uk/rest-breaks-work) says workers over 18 usually have the right to:

- one uninterrupted 20 minute rest break during the working day if they work more than 6 hours a day
- 11 hours rest between working days, so a carer finishing at 8pm should not start again before 7am the next day
- either an uninterrupted 24 hours without any work each week, or 48 hours each fortnight

A contract can give more than this. In home care the risk is that breaks exist on paper but disappear in practice, for example when a late evening call is followed by an early morning one. Check your rotas for short gaps between shifts as well as for missing breaks.

## How to spot when a carer is struggling

The HSE's page on [signs of stress](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/signs.htm) says that if workers start acting differently, it can be a sign they are stressed. In individuals, look for:

- taking more time off or arriving later
- mood swings, being withdrawn or more nervous
- loss of motivation, commitment and confidence
- being more tearful, sensitive or aggressive than usual

In a team, the HSE lists arguments, higher staff turnover, more reports of stress, more sickness absence, decreased performance and more complaints and grievances.

Acas adds that signs of poor mental health can be hard to spot, because everyone has different signs and many people find it difficult to talk about their mental health. That is especially true for carers who rarely come into the office. Its advice is to ask workers regularly how they are doing and help them to be open about how they feel. The sooner you know, the sooner you can help.

These signs can have other causes, so avoid assumptions. Have a private conversation, listen, and agree what support might help. The HSE suggests encouraging someone to see their GP if you are worried, and taking action if something at work has caused the problem.

## A practical wellbeing plan for your agency

Skills for Care's [staff wellbeing, rewards and recognition](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Retaining-your-workforce/Building-a-workforce-that-stays/Staff-wellbeing-rewards-and-recognition.aspx) guidance sets out practical steps that work well in a home care agency:

1. **Make wellbeing part of supervision.** Skills for Care recommends building wellbeing and recognition conversations into supervision and team meetings, to promote openness and reduce stigma. Add a standing wellbeing question to every supervision. Our guide to [supervision and appraisal for care workers](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html) covers how to run supervision well.
2. **Offer accessible support early.** Skills for Care suggests offering counselling or an employee assistance programme and responding promptly to concerns. Make sure every carer knows what support exists and how to reach it, including out of hours.
3. **Equip your managers and seniors.** Train the people who supervise carers to identify stress, respond with compassion and recognise contributions consistently and fairly.
4. **Manage workload and plan fair rotas.** Monitor pressures, build in realistic travel time, and share evening, weekend and short-notice work fairly. Skills for Care recommends consistent rotas and hours where possible, and applying rotas, workloads, pay and overtime consistently.
5. **Offer flexibility.** Consider individual needs, such as caring responsibilities, and provide flexibility during personal challenges.
6. **Debrief after difficult visits.** After a death, a safeguarding concern, an incident or an aggressive visit, check in with the carer the same day if you can.
7. **Recognise and thank people.** Skills for Care notes that while pay is important, everyday actions that show staff they are valued can make a real difference to whether people choose to stay.
8. **Listen and act.** Use staff surveys, stay conversations and [exit interviews](/blog/exit-interviews-care-workers.html) to find out what is wearing carers down, then tell the team what you are changing.

Some agencies go further with peer support. Skills for Care's [wellbeing champions practical approach](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Support-for-leaders-and-managers/Practical-approach-toolkits/Practical-approach-Wellbeing-champions.aspx) describes a peer-led model, developed in Staffordshire and Stoke-on-Trent, where trained champions encourage open conversations and make wellbeing support easier to find. Its [wellbeing resource finder](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Support-for-leaders-and-managers/Managing-people/Wellbeing/Workforce-wellbeing-finder/Wellbeing-resource-finder.aspx) groups free resources on topics such as mental health, grief and bereavement, and physical wellbeing.

## Supporting carers with health conditions

Wellbeing also means supporting carers who have a disability or a long-term physical or mental health condition. Make reasonable adjustments where they are needed, such as changes to hours, runs or duties, and keep health information confidential.

The government's [Access to Work](https://www.gov.uk/access-to-work) scheme can help someone get or stay in work if they have a physical or mental health condition or disability. It can include grants for practical support and help with managing mental health at work. GOV.UK is clear that Access to Work will not pay for reasonable adjustments, which are the changes an employer must legally make. When a carer is off sick, check the current [Statutory Sick Pay](https://www.gov.uk/statutory-sick-pay) rules on GOV.UK and any sick pay scheme in your own contracts.

## Do not forget the registered manager

Registered managers and care coordinators carry a lot of pressure too, from on-call phones to inspection preparation. CQC expects support to reach the whole workforce, and a stretched manager cannot support anyone else for long. Skills for Care's [wellbeing pages for managers](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Support-for-leaders-and-managers/Managing-people/Wellbeing/Wellbeing.aspx) include a bite-sized wellbeing guide for registered managers, webinars, registered manager networks and peer support groups.

## Why wellbeing matters for retention

Wellbeing and retention are closely linked. Skills for Care's [recruitment and retention pilots final report](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/About-us/Recruitment-and-retention-pilots-final-report.pdf) found that providers widely used, and most often rated as very effective, actions such as regular supervision or one-to-ones, wellbeing support, flexible working and staff recognition. The report suggests that preventative and relational approaches play a more important role in retention than reactive measures.

In other words, the carer who feels supported this month is the carer who is still on your rota next year. Our guides to [care worker retention ideas](/blog/care-worker-retention-ideas.html), [reducing carer turnover](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html) and [employee engagement in home care](/blog/employee-engagement-home-care.html) take this further.

## A simple wellbeing checklist

| Area | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Stress risk assessment | Done, written down if you have five or more workers, and reviewed when things change |
| Rotas | Realistic travel time, legal rest breaks and daily rest, fair sharing of unsocial hours |
| Supervision | A wellbeing question in every supervision, with actions followed up |
| Support | Carers know how to reach the office, on-call and any counselling or assistance service |
| Difficult visits | A same-day check-in after deaths, incidents, safeguarding concerns or aggression |
| Health conditions | Reasonable adjustments considered, Access to Work signposted, information kept confidential |
| Feedback | Staff surveys, stay conversations and exit interviews, with changes shared with the team |
| Managers | Registered manager and seniors have their own supervision and peer support |
