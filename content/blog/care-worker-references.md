Most home care agencies know references matter. Fewer have a process that holds up when an inspector opens a staff file. A single "she was fine" email from a personal address, with no date and no link to a previous care employer, is a common gap. So is a carer who started work while one reference was "on its way".

This guide is for registered managers, owners and recruitment coordinators at UK home care agencies registered with CQC. It covers what the regulations ask for, who to ask, when to ask, how to check what comes back, and what to do when a reference is missing, basic or worrying. It is not legal advice. For anything contentious, take HR or legal advice before you act.

## What CQC actually requires

The legal starting point is [Regulation 19: Fit and proper persons employed](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-19). It says staff must be of good character and have the qualifications, competence, skills and experience their work needs. CQC's guidance says providers must have effective recruitment and selection procedures, and that the information about candidates set out in Schedule 3 of the regulations **must be confirmed before they are employed**.

[Schedule 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/2936/schedule/3) lists that information. Two items are about references directly:

- **Item 4.** Satisfactory evidence of conduct in previous employment concerned with the provision of services relating to health or social care, or to children or vulnerable adults.
- **Item 5.** Where the person has previously been employed in a position whose duties involved work with children or vulnerable adults, satisfactory verification, so far as reasonably practicable, of the reason why that employment ended.

A third item shapes how you use them:

- **Item 7.** A full employment history, together with a satisfactory written explanation of any gaps in employment.

Notice what the regulations do not say. They do not set a number of references, and they do not say "the last employer". They point you to **previous care, or similar, employment** and to **why it ended**. An applicant who worked in a care home three years ago and in a shop since has a care employer you need to hear from, even though it is not their most recent job.

On good character, CQC's guidance says providers should make every effort to gather all available information, and expects the process to take account of honesty, trust, reliability and respect. If you decide an applicant is suitable despite information about anything in Schedule 3, CQC says your reasons should be recorded.

References sit alongside the other Schedule 3 checks, not in place of them. Acas puts it simply in its guidance on [what employers can say in a reference](https://www.acas.org.uk/providing-a-job-reference/what-employers-can-say-in-a-reference): a reference is not a substitute for making other checks, such as [checking a job applicant's right to work](https://www.gov.uk/check-job-applicant-right-to-work). For the full list, see our [care worker recruitment checklist](/blog/care-worker-recruitment-checklist.html) and our guide to the [DBS check for care workers](/blog/dbs-check-for-care-workers.html).

## Who to ask

Start from the employment history, not from the names the applicant offers. Read the history first, then decide which references you need.

1. **Every previous care or similar role.** Under Schedule 3, conduct in health or social care work, or work with children or vulnerable adults, is what you need evidence of. Ask each of those employers, starting with the most recent.
2. **The most recent employer, whatever the sector.** It gives you the freshest view of reliability and conduct, and helps confirm the history.
3. **Someone who knows them well, for a first job.** Where an applicant has little or no work history, such as a school or college leaver, Acas notes a character reference can come from someone like a manager, a mentor or someone they have volunteered for. It is useful evidence of character. It is not evidence of conduct in care employment, because there was none.

A good rule: **the referee should be the organisation, not a friend who worked there.** Ask for a named manager or HR contact at a work email address or the organisation's main phone number. Be cautious with personal email addresses and mobile numbers alone. Most are genuine, but you cannot tell from the email.

Many agencies ask for at least two references as their own policy. That is a sensible floor, but the Schedule 3 test is about evidence, not a count. Two references from non-care jobs do not cover a care role from last year.

## When to ask

Acas recommends asking for references at the final stage of recruitment, when making a job offer. Its page on [when an employer must give a reference](https://www.acas.org.uk/providing-a-job-reference) says that before approaching a current employer, the recruiting employer should have:

- made a conditional job offer
- the applicant's permission to request references
- made clear at the application stage whether a work or a character reference is needed

Acas explains why. If you ask a current employer before an offer, the applicant's employer may learn they are job hunting, which can count against them.

In home care this creates a timing pressure. CQC expects Schedule 3 information to be confirmed before employment, and you want the carer on the rota soon. The answer is to send reference requests **the day you make the conditional offer**, alongside the DBS application, and chase them on a set schedule. ## What to ask a referee

A free-text "please provide a reference" invites a two-line reply. A short, structured request gets something you can use. Acas has a [reference request letter template](https://www.acas.org.uk/reference-request-letter-template) you can adapt. For a care role, ask the referee to confirm or comment on:

- **Their own name, role and relationship** to the applicant, and how long they supervised them.
- **Job title and dates of employment.** Check these against the application.
- **The main duties**, especially personal care, medication support or lone working.
- **Reason for leaving.** For roles working with vulnerable adults or children, Schedule 3 item 5 asks you to verify this.
- **Conduct and reliability.** Honesty, timekeeping, attitude to the people supported, and working with colleagues. These map to the honesty, trust, reliability and respect CQC mentions.
- **Current, relevant disciplinary matters**, if their policy allows them to share them.
- **Any safeguarding concerns** they are aware of. Ask plainly.
- **Whether they would re-employ** the person in a similar role, and if not, why.

### Keep it lawful and fair

Acas notes that employers must follow discrimination law when requesting and checking references, not only when giving them. Do not ask about protected characteristics, and do not use absence related to disability, pregnancy or maternity, or other family leave, against an applicant. Ask only for the information you need for the role.

References are personal data. Tell applicants in your privacy notice how you collect, use and store them, and keep them securely in the staff file. The ICO's [employment information](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/employment/) guidance is the place to check your approach.

## How to check what comes back

A reference arriving is not the same as a reference being checked. For each one, before you file it:

- **Match it to the history.** Do the job title and dates agree with what the applicant told you? A difference of a few weeks may be innocent. A role that does not appear at all needs a conversation.
- **Check it came from the employer.** Look at the sender's email domain, the letterhead and the signature. If anything looks odd, phone the organisation on a number you have found yourself, not one supplied with the reference.
- **Make a short verification call** for care employers. A two-minute call to confirm the reference was sent, and to ask anything left blank, often tells you more than the form. Note who you spoke to, when, and what they said.
- **Read for what is missing.** A reference that avoids conduct, or will not say whether they would re-employ, is not necessarily bad. It is a reason to ask a follow-up question.
- **Reconcile gaps.** Schedule 3 item 7 asks for a satisfactory written explanation of any gaps in employment. Get it from the applicant, in writing, and keep it with the references.
- **Sign it off.** Record who reviewed the references, on what date, and the decision. That record is what an inspector wants to see.

## When references are missing, basic or poor

### The employer has closed, or will not reply

Acas notes that if an organisation has gone out of business, an applicant can ask the recruiting employer to accept a reference from someone else, such as a former manager, or from a different employer. If a care employer simply will not respond, chase it in writing more than once and record each attempt. Schedule 3 item 5 says "so far as reasonably practicable", so your file needs to show what you tried. Then decide whether you have enough other evidence of conduct, and write down your reasons.

### Only a basic reference

Acas says some employers have a policy of giving only basic references, such as job title and dates. That is common in larger organisations and is not a red flag on its own. Accept it for the facts it confirms, then look for further evidence of conduct, for example from another care employer or a former line manager, and record how you were satisfied.

### A worrying reference

Do not ignore it, and do not reject someone on a single vague comment either. Talk to the applicant, give them a chance to explain, and if needed go back to the referee with specific questions. Acas says a reference must be accurate and fair, and that any opinions should be supported by evidence. If the concern is about harm or risk of harm to people, follow your safeguarding procedure. The DBS guidance on [barring referrals](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/dbs-referrals-guidance--2) explains when an employer has a legal duty to refer.

If you decide to go ahead despite a concern, CQC's Regulation 19 guidance says your reasons should be recorded for future reference. Think about extra supervision or spot checks during the carer's [probation period](/blog/care-worker-probation-period.html).

## Giving references for carers who leave you

Your agency will be asked for references too. Acas says there is usually no legal duty to give one, but if you do, it must be accurate and fair. A clear policy helps: who in the agency can give references, what you include, and how you handle safeguarding concerns, which belong in your DBS and safeguarding processes as well as in a fair reference. Be consistent. Giving a glowing reference to avoid a difficult conversation can put the next agency's clients at risk.

## A quick references checklist

| Step | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Application | Full employment history with dates, gaps explained in writing, consent to contact referees |
| Conditional offer | Reference requests sent the same day, to work addresses or verified phone numbers |
| Who | Every previous care or similar employer, plus the most recent employer |
| What | Structured questions covering dates, duties, reason for leaving, conduct and safeguarding |
| Verify | Dates matched to the history, source confirmed, short call to care employers |
| Gaps and problems | Each attempt and follow-up recorded; reasons recorded for any decision to proceed |
| Before day one | All references checked and signed off, with the Schedule 3 checks complete |
| File | References, notes of calls, gap explanations and the sign-off kept in the staff file |
