If you only advertise for experienced carers, you are fishing in a small pond that every other agency in your area is fishing in too. People who have never worked in care are a much bigger pool. Many of them already have the qualities you want most, and the rest can be taught through a good induction.

This guide covers why recruiting people with no experience is a sound choice, how to reach them, what the regulations still require, how to assess them fairly, and how to support them through their first months so they stay.

## Why recruit people with no care experience

Skills for Care is clear on this. Its guide [What values do I need to work in social care?](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Documents/Recruitment-and-retention/Careers-in-care/What-values-do-I-need-to-work-in-social-care.pdf) says that, depending on the role, you do not always need qualifications or previous work experience in the sector to get a job in social care. What is really important is having the right values, behaviours and attitudes. Its [Getting started](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Careers-in-care/Getting-started/Getting-Started.aspx) page for job seekers says the same thing.

The government's [care workforce pathway](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/care-workforce-pathway-for-adult-social-care) builds this into the career structure for adult social care in England. Its ['new to care' role category](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/care-workforce-pathway-for-adult-social-care/new-to-care-role-category) is described as a starting point for a career in social care, designed for individuals with no experience in the sector, and it names care in people's own homes as one of the settings. It focuses on building a foundational level of competence to ensure safe practice.

So an agency that hires people new to care is not lowering the bar. It is recruiting for the part that is hardest to teach, and then training the rest. That is the idea behind [values-based recruitment](/blog/values-based-recruitment-social-care.html).

There are practical benefits too:

- **A bigger pool.** You are no longer competing only for the same experienced carers as every other agency nearby.
- **Fewer habits to unlearn.** A new starter learns your care plans, your policies and your way of working from day one.
- **Loyalty.** People often remember who gave them their first chance in a new career.

The trade-off is time. Someone new to care needs a fuller induction, more shadowing and closer supervision before they visit anyone alone. Plan for that in your rota and your budget before you recruit.

## Who to look for

Look for evidence of care values in everyday life, not in a CV full of care jobs. The care workforce pathway lists the values for the 'new to care' role as kind, compassionate and empathetic; honest, trustworthy and reliable; respect; courageous and principled; seeing the whole person; flexible, open and learning; and proud and positive.

People who often show those values without a care job title include:

- people who have looked after a relative or a friend;
- volunteers, for example at a community group, charity shop, faith group or sports club;
- people from customer-facing jobs such as retail, hospitality, call centres and hairdressing;
- parents returning to work, who may want hours that fit around school;
- people changing career who want work that feels more worthwhile.

Ask applicants for examples from any part of their life. "Tell me about a time you helped someone who was struggling" works whether the answer comes from a shop floor or a family kitchen.

## Writing an advert that reaches them

People with no experience often assume care jobs are not for them. Your advert has to tell them otherwise, in plain words. Our guide to [writing a care worker job advert](/blog/care-worker-job-advert.html) covers the whole advert. For new-to-care applicants, make sure it says:

- **"No experience needed"** near the top, in the job title or the first line.
- **What training you give** and when: induction, the Care Certificate standards, shadowing with experienced colleagues and who they can call for help.
- **What the work involves**, honestly. Personal care, medication support, travel between visits and lone working are all part of home care. Being honest now saves an early leaver later.
- **Pay, hours and travel.** Say how you pay, including how travel time is handled, and whether a car and licence are needed for your rounds. Our guide to [minimum wage and travel time in home care](/blog/national-minimum-wage-travel-time-home-care.html) explains why this matters.
- **How to apply**, in as few steps as possible. Many good applicants are applying from a phone.

## The checks do not change

Recruiting someone new to care does not change your legal duties. [Regulation 19](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-19) requires the people you employ to be of good character, to have the qualifications, competence, skills and experience necessary for the work (which, for a new starter, is what your induction and training must provide), and to be able, after reasonable adjustments, to do the tasks that are intrinsic to the role. [Schedule 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/2936/schedule/3) lists the information you must have.

In practice, the same file applies to every carer:

- **Proof of identity and right to work.** Follow the GOV.UK guide to [checking a job applicant's right to work](https://www.gov.uk/check-job-applicant-right-to-work).
- **The right DBS check.** Use GOV.UK's tool to [find out which DBS check is right](https://www.gov.uk/find-out-dbs-check), and see our guide to the [DBS check for care workers](/blog/dbs-check-for-care-workers.html).
- **A full employment history, with gaps explained in writing.** For someone who has been at school, college, caring for family or out of work, the explanation is the evidence. Record it.
- **References.** Where someone has little or no work history, a character reference from someone like a teacher, a mentor or a person they have volunteered for can help. Where they have worked with children or vulnerable adults before, even briefly, seek evidence of conduct from that work. Our guide to [care worker references](/blog/care-worker-references.html) sets out who to ask.
- **Health information** relevant to the role, gathered fairly.

The full list is in our [care worker recruitment checklist](/blog/care-worker-recruitment-checklist.html), and our guide to [safer recruitment in domiciliary care](/blog/safer-recruitment-domiciliary-care.html) shows what CQC expects to see in the file.

## Assessing potential, not experience

Without a care history to discuss, your interview has to find out how someone thinks and what they value. Our [care worker interview questions](/blog/care-worker-interview-questions.html) include values and scenario questions you can use. For applicants new to care:

- **Ask for examples from any part of life.** Caring for a parent, calming an upset customer or covering for a colleague all show something.
- **Use simple scenarios.** "You arrive and the person says they do not want a wash today. What do you do?" There is no care jargon to know, only judgement and respect to show.
- **Score against the same values every time,** so a confident talker does not beat a quieter person with better instincts.
- **Explain the job plainly** and watch how they react to the hard parts, such as personal care, early starts and working alone.

Skills for Care has also published [A Question of Care](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/resources/documents/Recruitment-support/Application-and-selection-process/A-Question-of-Care-guidance.pdf), an interactive challenge based on real care scenarios that gives candidates a personal profile mapped to care values. Its guidance says the profile should not be used on its own to decide whether someone is suitable. At the time of writing, Skills for Care's Getting started page says the quiz is being updated, so check it is available before building it into your process.

## Induction and supervision before they work alone

This is where recruiting new-to-care staff succeeds or fails. CQC's guidance on [Regulation 18](https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-18) says providers must have an induction that prepares staff for their role, and that staff must be supervised until they can demonstrate the competence to carry out the role unsupervised.

For people new to care, most agencies build the induction around the [Care Certificate standards](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Developing-your-workforce/Care-Certificate/Care-Certificate.aspx). Skills for Care says the standards can be used if you are new to care and to inform what induction covers. They were updated in March 2025, and there are now 16, including a new standard on awareness of learning disability and autism. Our guide to the [Care Certificate for home care agencies](/blog/care-certificate-home-care-agencies.html) explains the standards, direct supervision and sign-off in detail, and our [Oliver McGowan training guide](/blog/oliver-mcgowan-training-home-care.html) covers the learning disability and autism training requirement.

A sensible plan for a new-to-care carer includes:

1. **Office induction** covering your policies, safeguarding, lone working, how to read a care plan and how to raise a concern.
2. **The [mandatory training](/blog/mandatory-training-care-workers.html)** your agency requires before any visit, such as moving and handling, infection prevention and control, and medication support where the role includes it.
3. **Shadowing** on real visits with an experienced carer, with the person's agreement, on a range of calls.
4. **Observed practice and sign-off,** standard by standard, by someone competent to assess.
5. **A gradual move to working alone,** starting with calls the carer has already shadowed, with spot checks after.

Skills for Care's [induction toolkit](https://www.skillsforcare.org.uk/Recruitment-and-retention/Induction-toolkit/Induction-toolkit.aspx) has checklists for each stage, and our guide to [onboarding new carers](/blog/onboarding-new-carers-home-care.html) walks through the steps from offer to first visit.

Do not rush this to fill a gap in the rota. A new carer sent out alone too soon is a risk to the people they visit, and a likely early leaver.

## Keeping them through the first months

New starters are most likely to leave early, when the work is still unfamiliar. Simple things help:

- **A named buddy** they can call between visits.
- **Regular check-ins** in the first weeks, not only formal [supervision](/blog/supervision-appraisal-care-workers.html).
- **Fair, predictable rotas,** with new starters given a manageable round, not every awkward call.
- **A clear [probation period](/blog/care-worker-probation-period.html)** with honest feedback and a clear point at which they are confirmed.
- **Recognition** when they get something right, especially early on.

Our guides to [care worker retention ideas](/blog/care-worker-retention-ideas.html) and [reducing carer turnover](/blog/carer-turnover-home-care.html) go further.

## A checklist for recruiting people new to care

| Stage | What to have in place |
|---|---|
| Advert | "No experience needed", the training you give, honest description of the work, pay, travel and how to apply |
| Application | Full employment history, gaps explained in writing, examples of care values from any part of life |
| Interview | Values and scenario questions scored the same way for every applicant |
| Checks | Identity, right to work, the right DBS check, references (character references where history is thin), health information |
| Induction | Policies, mandatory training, Care Certificate standards and Oliver McGowan training |
| Supervision | Shadowing, observed practice and sign-off before any unsupervised visit |
| First months | Buddy, regular check-ins, a fair rota, probation reviews and supervision |
