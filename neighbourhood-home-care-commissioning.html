Neighbourhood home care commissioning: how agencies get ready

On 12 November 2026 the government asked councils in England to start buying home care by neighbourhood, with one or two main providers in each small area. This guide sets out what was actually announced, what has not been decided yet, and the practical steps an agency can take now. The second half explains how homecareOS helps you win and run that kind of contract, using only what is in the product today.

By Philip Martin · Founder, homecareOS · Published 17 November 2026

Part 1: the guide

What was announced

On 12 November 2026 the Department of Health and Social Care published a press release, More familiar carers and less travel under new social care plans. The same day, Minister Alison McGovern set out the thinking in her keynote at the National Children and Adult Services Conference.

The idea is simple. Today many councils buy individual visits from lots of providers spread across a wide area, so a person can see different carers on different days and care workers spend a large part of the day driving. Instead, councils are encouraged to commission home care across smaller geographical areas, with one or two main providers serving each neighbourhood. Care workers would spend their day in a much smaller area, and people would have a better chance of seeing the same carers regularly.

The speech ties this to the wider neighbourhood health programme: health and care neighbourhoods should line up, with GPs, community nurses, social care and specialist teams working together. The minister describes it as a step towards a national care service.

What has not been decided yet

It is easy to read more into an announcement than it says. Five things are worth being clear about.

  • It is encouragement, not a requirement. Councils will be encouraged to commission this way. The government says it will work with councils over the coming months to track and support their progress, and the minister wants all councils to move over in time. No targets or deadlines were given.
  • The detail comes next year. The press release says guidance and toolkits will be published in the spring. The speech says models and materials will follow early next year.
  • There is no payment model yet. Dr Jane Townson of the Homecare Association, quoted in the press release, calls for fees that cover the cost of care, predictable work, and payment for whole rounds rather than contact minutes. That is the trade body's ask. The government has not said how neighbourhood contracts will be paid.
  • It reads as England only. The speech talks about English councils, and adult social care is devolved. If you work in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland, watch what your own government and councils say.
  • Nothing has been said about provider data or reporting. Neither page sets out what evidence a council will ask for. It is reasonable to expect tenders to ask about continuity and travel, because those are the stated aims, but nobody knows the exact questions yet.

What providers will be asked to do

The minister's speech is the clearest statement so far. Providers working within a defined neighbourhood would be expected to:

  • plan sensible routes;
  • offer predictable hours to their staff;
  • invest in training;
  • account properly for travel time.

The aim she gives is better use of the workforce, not a smaller one: a small, familiar team around each person, more time with them and less rushing between calls. The press release adds that people should still have a real choice over who supports them, a point the Homecare Association makes strongly.

Travel time is already a pay question. GOV.UK guidance on working hours for the minimum wage treats a care worker driving from one client to another between appointments as working time. Travel from home to the first client generally is not. "Properly accounting for travel time" starts with getting this right.

Nine steps to get ready

The guidance is months away, but the evidence a council is likely to want takes months to build. None of the steps below commits you to anything, and each one is useful even if your council never changes how it buys care. This is our advice, not government guidance.

  1. Map where your work is now

    Put every client and every carer on a map by postcode district. Look for the places where you already have a cluster of clients and carers who live nearby, and the places where you are thin and travel a long way for one or two calls.

  2. Decide which neighbourhoods you could credibly run

    Being one of one or two main providers in an area means taking most of its care, including the calls that are hard to staff. Be honest about where you have the carers to do that. Ask your council how it intends to draw its neighbourhoods, and whether they will follow the local NHS neighbourhood teams.

  3. Start measuring continuity now

    Pick a measure you can explain and keep using it, for example the share of each person's visits delivered by their most regular carer, and how many different carers each person saw in a month. The announcement gives no national target, so the point is to show your own record and that it is improving. History cannot be produced after the event, so start counting before the tender arrives.

  4. Measure travel honestly

    Know how much of your carers' day goes on driving, as hours and as a share of the time they spend with people. You cannot show that a neighbourhood model would cut travel without a starting point.

  5. Check how you pay travel time

    Look at whether travel between calls is paid, at what rate, and whether every carer still clears the minimum wage once travel time counts as working time. A council that wants providers to account properly for travel time will look at this.

  6. Build rounds, not lists of visits

    Group calls into rounds that stay inside an area and repeat week to week. Rounds give carers predictable hours, which is one of the things the minister named, and they show a commissioner that you plan routes on purpose.

  7. Get your delivery record in order

    Councils already ask about visits delivered, visits missed, and hours delivered against hours commissioned. Make sure you can produce those figures for any period, per council, without a week of spreadsheet work.

  8. Recruit for the patch

    A neighbourhood contract works best with carers who live near the people they visit. When you advertise, say where the work is, and favour applicants who live in the areas you want to cover.

  9. Talk to your commissioner early

    Ask what your council is planning and when. Share what you can already show. Then watch for the spring guidance before you restructure anything: the evidence above is worth collecting whatever model your council chooses.

Part 2: how homecareOS helps

How homecareOS helps you win and run neighbourhood contracts

Everything in this section is in homecareOS today. Where something needs switching on for your agency, we say so.

Areas: your patch, drawn by postcode

Divide your patch into named areas, each defined by postcode districts such as WR1 or WR5. Carers and clients are assigned to an area, and the rota and visits screens can then be viewed one area at a time, so a coordinator plans one neighbourhood without the rest of the agency in the way.

Runs: repeatable rounds

A run is a repeatable round of calls. Save one from the rota by selecting a morning's visits, or build it from scratch, then apply it to a day. Suggest runs looks at your weekly plan and proposes chains of calls in the same part of the day where each stop is reachable from the last, for your coordinator to accept or ignore. Runs are switched on for an agency on request.

Continuity you can show

The Care continuity report shows, for any date range, how many visits each person had, how many different carers they saw, who their main carer was and what share of their visits that carer delivered. It exports to CSV.

When you suggest a carer or look for cover, homecareOS ranks candidates with a fixed score out of 100: continuity counts for 45 points, spare contracted hours for 30 and travel to the address for 25. A carer who is not free scores nothing. If you move to homecareOS from another system, our team can load your past visit history, so carers who already know someone are not ranked as strangers. Those past visits count until the person has four weeks of visits recorded in homecareOS.

Travel, measured and paid

Every night homecareOS works out the drive time and distance between each carer's consecutive calls at different addresses. Those journeys become mileage claims and travel time on the carer's timesheet. In payroll settings you choose whether travel time is unpaid, paid at the carer's rate, or paid at a separate hourly rate, and whether payslips show travel as one total or journey by journey.

The Round efficiency report shows travel hours by carer and by month, and travel as a share of contact time. It leaves that share out for any carer with fewer than five journeys, because a figure from a handful of trips would mislead.

Your delivery record, for any period

The Operational report counts completion as visits delivered divided by visits due, so a visit still to come never drags your rate down and a missed one always counts against it. The Care hours report sets hours commissioned against hours delivered. Both run for any date range and export to CSV. Carers clock in by GPS, QR code or NFC tag, so the times behind those figures are recorded at the door.

A portal for your commissioners

Invite a council's commissioners to their own login. They see visit completion, missed visits, hours delivered against hours commissioned and the invoices raised to them, run reports for any dates and export them to CSV. Since 16 November 2026 the database itself limits each commissioner login to the people their council pays for, and to the visits under that council's own services. Read more on the commissioner portal page.

Questions agencies are asking

Do councils have to commission home care by neighbourhood?

No. The Department of Health and Social Care says councils will be encouraged to commission home care across smaller geographical areas. It is not a legal requirement. The government says it will work with councils over the coming months to track and support their progress, and the minister has said she wants all councils to move over in time.

When will the neighbourhood home care guidance be published?

The press release of 12 November 2026 says new guidance and toolkits will be published in the spring. In her speech the same day, the minister said she plans to publish models and materials early next year, meaning early 2027.

Does neighbourhood commissioning apply in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland?

The announcement reads as England only. The minister's speech talks about English councils, and adult social care is devolved. Agencies in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland should follow what their own governments and councils say.

Will councils pay for whole rounds instead of contact minutes?

The government has not set a payment model. Paying for whole rounds rather than contact minutes is what the Homecare Association has called for, quoted in the press release. It is the trade body's ask, not government policy.

Does travel time between visits count for the National Minimum Wage?

Yes. GOV.UK guidance on calculating the minimum wage treats time spent travelling between appointments, such as a care worker driving from one client to the next, as working time. Travel from home to the first client generally is not.
